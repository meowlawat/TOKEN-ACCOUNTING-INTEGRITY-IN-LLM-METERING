Accounting Integrity in Metered LLM-Serving Infrastructure: Bounded
Evidence for Client-Side Under-Payment
HARDIK∗, Department of Computer Science and Data Analytics, Indian Institute of Technology Patna,
India and Vivekananda Institute of Professional Studies – Technical Campus, India
NATASHA KAILA, Vivekananda Institute of Professional Studies – Technical Campus, India
Metered AI-inference services are deployed systems in which computational value is streamed to the client before the
charge is settled, usage is produced at runtime, and settlement is often asynchronous – placing the usage-metering
and billing boundary inside the security perimeter of the AI-serving infrastructure itself, not outside it. We study
the integrity of that boundary against an authenticated client that seeks to pay less than it owes for inference it
received. Modeling a metered request as a lifecycle with a single integrity property, 𝑉 (𝑟 ) ≤ 𝑁 (𝑟 ), we localize failures
to three architectural placements: state synchronization (B0), commitment timing (M1), and usage authority (M2).
We apply bounded exhaustive model checking in TLA+/TLC across ten conﬁgurations and four invariants, and
validate the model predictions on a testbed spanning three execution topologies, three accounting families including
an asynchronous event pipeline, and a third-party serving stack. Three results follow. Reservation and abort-safe
ﬁnalization are orthogonal security properties: each secures only one of solvency and accounting integrity, and only
their conjunction secures both. Correct usage computation does not imply correct billing: an architecture that
recounts usage accurately but bills the client-declared value leaks 58.3% of delivered value in a controlled setting and
69.0% against a real serving stack, while server-authoritative billing eliminates the leak. Asynchronous settlement
reproduces over-serving under strictly sequential arrivals with no concurrency at all, showing that the synchronization
condition concerns not concurrency but whether authorization is atomically coupled to economic commitment on
the authorizing path; this persists under a network-separated two-machine deployment, while a settlement-conﬁrmed
serialization control shows none. We state model-level security conditions separately from the implementation patterns
that satisfy them, report that the strongest defense against usage-authority manipulation costs under 0.1% of end-
to-end time with real inference, and delimit the formal model, extended with a second, causal-ordering speciﬁcation
of the asynchronous settlement mechanism. The contribution is a systematic security analysis of deployed AI-serving
infrastructure: B0 is a known baseline, and M1/M2 systematize, formalize, and measure mechanisms with prior
individual precedent.

1 INTRODUCTION
LLM inference APIs bill for tokens that are produced at runtime and streamed to the client before the charge
is settled, which places the metering and billing path inside the attack surface of the AI-serving system.
The billed unit, the token, is produced at runtime by an opaque, streamed, long-running computation
delivered to a remote client over a network; the quantity is a multi-category vector; and the usage record
that determines the charge is itself produced by the serving system while the response is still in ﬂight – so the
authorization, execution, and settlement paths carry security-critical state, making the usage-metering and
settlement subsystem part of the AI-serving system’s security boundary rather than a peripheral accounting
concern external to it. Mistakes in how that state is partitioned across components can produce integrity
failures even when every individual component computes usage correctly, which is why this paper studies
the accounting lifecycle itself: its states, its transitions, the conditions under which it is safe, and what
happens empirically when those conditions are broken.
This is a structural property of metered computational services generally, not an accident of one imple-
mentation, which is why the lifecycle we study is stated over the request lifecycle of a metered service rather
than tied to language-model inference. Such services sell work whose quantity is not known when the work is
authorized. A client asks for computation, the service performs and delivers it, and only afterwards does the
system learn what to charge. Between those two moments the service holds economic state that is authori-
tative for billing but not yet durable, and that state is read, written, and reconciled by several components:
an authorization path, an execution path that delivers value incrementally, and a settlement path that may
run asynchronously behind both. Whether the service can be relied on to charge for what it delivered is
therefore a question about distributed state management that the LLM case above makes concrete and
security-relevant, not merely an accounting-policy question. Existing security work on this surface runs in
three directions, all of which assume the client is honest, impersonated, or irrelevant. Provider-side auditing
asks whether a dishonest provider can inﬂate hidden token counts [ 7, 16, 36, 72, 73]. Economic-abuse work
asks whether a third party can drain a victim’s budget [ 21, 40, 59, 74]. Attestation work asks whether an
intermediary can misroute requests or forge provenance [ 30, 77]. The remaining quadrant is a legitimate,
authenticated client that under-pays, and that is what we study (Table 1).
The individual mechanisms have precedents in isolation, surveyed in Section 3 and, for ﬁve independently
developed metering gateways, collected as public reports in Section 3.5; none of those systems was tested
by us, and none is claimed here to be exploitable. What has not been available is a single model that places
them on one accounting lifecycle, checks it exhaustively, and states precisely when each defense is suﬃcient;
that uniﬁcation, veriﬁcation, and empirical validation is the contribution of this paper. Accordingly, B0 is
presented as a known baseline and M1 and M2 as systematization and measurement contributions.
These failures are usually discussed as unrelated bugs in unrelated systems; placed on one request lifecycle,
they are the same integrity property violated at three points, which yields three things no bug report does:

Contributions.
(1) An accounting-state model of a metered service request (Section 4), motivated by token-metered,
streamed LLM inference, connecting execution, value delivery, authorization, reservation, debit,
reconciliation, and refund, with a single integrity property, 𝑉 (𝑟 ) ≤ 𝑁 (𝑟 ), that admits legitimate

2 THREAT MODEL AND SECURITY PROPERTY
The provider is honest. The adversary is a client with valid credentials whose goal is to receive metered
inference and pay less than it owes. It controls six things, each exercised by our harness: request payloads,
including any client-declared usage ﬁelds; permitted headers; request concurrency; the connection lifecycle,
so it may abort at any moment; retries; and observation of its own responses and balance. It cannot
compromise TLS, reach the database or Redis, read the gateway ﬁlesystem, compromise the inference
backend, forge authenticated upstream metadata, or modify server code. This is an ordinary application-
layer adversary, not a symbolic cryptographic one – every leak we report is attributable to the provider’s
own accounting design, not a compromise.
2.1 Attacker knowledge
Our M2 harness builds each declared-usage manipulation by transforming the true server-side usage vector,
which hands the attacker more information than a real client has. Rather than leave that convenience
implicit, we grade every manipulation by the knowledge it actually needs. K0 is client-observable only: the
prompt sent, the response received, and the client’s own balance. K1 additionally requires provider-reported
usage, whatever the architecture returns. K2 requires oracle access to the exact server-side usage vector.
Under-reporting output or input, shaving a small constant, and declaring an inconsistent total are all
K0, since a client can count its own prompt and the tokens it received. Inﬂating the cached-input category
is K1, because the cached/uncached split is not visible in the response. Dropping hidden reasoning tokens
is K2, because that count is never exposed. The headline usage-authority result rests on a K0 manipulation
and does not depend on oracle knowledge; the two non-K0 manipulations are labeled and kept out of the
main claim.
2.2 Integrity property
For a request 𝑟 , let 𝑉 (𝑟 ) be the value actually delivered, 𝐷 (𝑟 ) the committed debits, 𝐹 (𝑟 ) the refunds, and
𝑁 (𝑟 ) = 𝐷 (𝑟 ) − 𝐹 (𝑟 ) the net committed debit. Under-payment and the integrity property are
Leak(𝑟 ) = 𝑉 (𝑟 ) − 𝑁 (𝑟 ), Integrity(𝑟 ) : 𝑉 (𝑟 ) ≤ 𝑁 (𝑟 ). (1)
The inequality is over the net debit so that reconciliation remains legitimate: a client that consumes less than
its reservation must get the diﬀerence back. A refund is legitimate only when it releases the unconsumed
portion,
𝐹 (𝑟 ) ≤ Res(𝑟 ) − 𝑉 (𝑟 ), (2)
and the vulnerable “refund the whole reservation on abort” architecture violates exactly this side condition.
2.3 Two properties, not one
Accounting integrity is not the only thing a metering system owes, and merging the two obscures the defense
analysis. We separate them:
• Accounting integrity, 𝑉 (𝑟 ) ≤ 𝑁 (𝑟 ): the client is never net-charged less than the value it received.
• Solvency: a request is served only when the system holds enough committed or reserved capacity
to cover it under its own policy.

3 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
LLM APIs meter multi-category token vectors, stream output incrementally, and settle charges after the
fact, each with its own literature; the gap we occupy sits between them. The study also draws on the
transaction and consistency literature that governs shared economic state, the escrow-style reservation
pattern for high-contention counters, and the veriﬁcation practice used to check stateful service designs.
3.1 Prior adversary framings
Pay-as-you-go pricing is a deﬁning feature of cloud services [ 6], enforced by deciding whether to admit work,
observing usage, and committing a charge – a placement of responsibilities that determines security [ 11]; the
failures studied here follow from where that placement sits in the request path, not from a defect in any single
component. The direction of error we study is under-payment by the client; the opposite direction, a platform
over-accounting resource usage against its own declared billing model, is also documented and is unrelated to
any client action [ 53]. Existing work covers the other three quadrants of Table 1. CoIn [ 72] shows reasoning
tokens invisible to the client can be counted and audited only with eﬀort; Sun et al. [ 73] argues opaque LLM
services need audit mechanisms because the billed quantity is unveriﬁable to the payer; and Hoque et al.
[36] measure how far a dishonest provider can overcharge before detection becomes likely – all three take
the client’s side against the provider, a direction we invert while keeping their observation that the billed
quantity is runtime-produced and hard to verify. The closest 2026 neighbor, PTIA, manipulates provider-
controlled generation to inﬂate output length and audits that behavior across 15 services; sequential pay-
per-token auditing likewise tests whether a provider misreports usage [ 7, 16]. Both occupy the opposite trust
direction: their provider may cheat an honest payer, whereas our provider is honest and the authenticated
client tries to under-pay. We therefore share the metered-inference boundary but neither reuse their threat
model nor claim to supersede their audits. A second group inﬂates somebody else’s bill: Kelly et al. [ 40]
deﬁne forced ﬁnancial exhaustion as a DoS-adjacent threat in serverless settings, consolidated by Dorsett
et al. [ 21]; OW ASP [59] ranks unbounded consumption among the top LLM-application risks; Sysdig Threat
Research Team [74] document stolen credentials resold for inference at the victim’s expense; and Shumailov
et al. [ 69] show inference cost can be driven adversarially. Logic ﬂaws in payment ﬂows are related: cashier-
as-a-service lets shoppers obtain goods without paying [ 78], and concurrency attacks on database-backed
applications exploit weak isolation for ﬁnancial eﬀect [ 79], but our billed quantity is produced during the
request rather than ﬁxed at checkout; complete mediation and fail-safe defaults [ 64] apply directly, and
the fail-closed recount path in Section 8 follows the second. Attestation and provenance work targets the
gateway path, not the endpoint: Guan [ 30] provide multi-hop attestation for LLM APIs, and Wang and
Tian [77] address forged routing claims with evidence-bound provenance, assuming a dishonest intermediary
rather than endpoint.

3.2 Generic mechanisms
Relying on untrusted input in a security decision is CWE-807 [ 54]. Concurrent quota races are well-
characterized, and Kettle [ 41] shows how narrow the exploitable window can be; the escrow transactional
method [ 57] is the classical treatment of high-contention counter updates and ancestor of the reserve-and-
reconcile pattern we evaluate. Streaming and client cancellation are speciﬁed behavior: server-sent events
[80] deﬁne a channel the client may close at any time, and HTTP semantics [ 25] leave an aborted response’s
consequences to the application.
B0 is a lost update per critiques of the ANSI isolation levels [ 13], sharpened by implementation-
independent isolation deﬁnitions [ 1]. Reserve-and-reconcile with a compensating refund resembles sagas
[27], and our asynchronous family’s idempotent, at-least-once handling follows standard messaging idempo-
tence [ 33]. Bailis et al. [ 8] analyzes whether an invariant like a non-negative balance needs coordination to
maintain; an authorization check against a bound does – the B0 condition – in the spirit of linearizability
[34]. The delay between value delivery and durable debit in the asynchronous family instances the staleness
eventually consistent stores expose [ 76], treated practically by Kleppmann [ 44].
3.3 Serving and tokenization
The billed quantity depends on a tokenizer: subword segmentation [ 67] and SentencePiece [ 45] underlie
today’s tokenizers, and engines legitimately disagree on the token count for identical text. Modern serving
systems batch and cache aggressively: PagedAttention/vLLM [ 46] established the preﬁx-reuse memory
management that makes the cached-token category observable and unstable in Section 10, and keep changing
execution structure – chunked preﬁll [ 2], disaggregated preﬁll/decoding [ 87], structured-program preﬁx reuse
[86], and speculative decoding [ 51] – so a metering subsystem cannot assume one ﬁxed execution model.
Token counts also depend on tokenizer and language [ 60]. Our real-stack validation uses llama.cpp [ 28]
serving SmolLM2-135M-Instruct [ 12], with tiktoken [ 58] among the tokenizers benchmarked.
3.4 Verification and validation practice
We specify the lifecycle in TLA+ [ 47, 48] and check it with TLC [ 84], in the model-checking tradition [ 19, 35,
38]. Using model checking to ﬁnd architectural defects that testing misses is established industrial practice
[56], and our experience matches the reported pattern: the checker’s value lay in refuting a speciﬁcation
we believed, not conﬁrming one. Surveys of formal-methods adoption [ 29, 83] and Lamport’s case for
specifying before coding [ 49] motivate the practice; implementation-level veriﬁcation has been achieved for
an OS kernel [ 43], a compiler [ 50], and distributed systems [ 32] at substantial proof eﬀort, while lightweight
methods validate against executable reference models [ 15] – an empirical study found bugs concentrated
where a veriﬁed speciﬁcation meets unveriﬁed components [ 26], the gap our evidence labels keep explicit.
Property-based testing [ 18] and metamorphic testing [ 17, 66] check properties and relations between outputs
without an oracle, as our price-scaling invariance check does; the oracle problem [ 9] is why we re-derive
every reported quantity independently rather than trust the system’s own bookkeeping. Fault injection is
established practice: chaos engineering perturbs running systems [ 10], lineage-driven injection guides fault
selection [ 3], and Elle checks recorded histories for isolation anomalies [ 42]; our nine lifecycle failures are

3.5 Evidence from deployed systems
The mechanisms studied here are not conﬁned to our testbed: Table 2 lists public, independently ﬁled reports
from ﬁve separately developed metering gateways and proxies showing the same architectural ingredient,
reviewed directly from the linked issue and pull-request pages, with each row stating what the report does
and does not establish.
This evidence is used carefully: we claim no listed system is exploitable and attempted nothing against
any of them – every measurement here comes from our own local testbed; presence of an architectural
ingredient is not presence of a vulnerability, which is why the table separates the two columns; and two of
the ﬁve reports are already ﬁxed by their own maintainers, so they are historical evidence the failure mode
occurs and is repairable, not a statement about those systems today. The table supports the weaker claim
we need: these accounting-lifecycle gaps occur in independently written gateway code, not only in ours.
3.6 The gap
Within our targeted corpus we found no study evaluating client-side under-payment under an honest-
provider threat model across representative metering architectures, with a common measurement and an
ablation of the enforcement primitives. This is a bounded negative from a targeted search. It does not
establish that no such work exists.

ﬁnds authentication and encryption frequently missing at the serving layer [ 37], and serving systems that
share concurrency, caches, and scheduling state across tenants have an independently documented, security-
relevant attack surface [ 68, 85] – the same shared runtime state this paper’s accounting lifecycle reasons
about, applied here to the economic rather than the conﬁdentiality or availability dimension. B0 is a known
baseline mechanism, included for comparison rather than as a contribution; M1 and M2 are not new attack
primitives either, but their systematization, exhaustive formal veriﬁcation, and measurement under the
LLM-serving usage model speciﬁcally – runtime-produced, multi-category, tokenizer-disputed – has not, to
our knowledge, been done before.

5 ARCHITECTURAL TAXONOMY
The three dimensions just distinguished architecturally are also experimentally distinguishable, not merely
conceptual: B0 is a non-atomic authorize-then-decrement on shared credit state, a known baseline used to
validate the measurement rig and supply a generic reference point, while M1 and M2 remain the lifecycle-
and authority-sensitive dimensions of Section 4.
5.1 On LLM specificity
We do not claim these mechanisms are possible only in LLM systems. Streaming responses, trusting client-
supplied values, and racing a counter all occur elsewhere. What is speciﬁc to LLM inference is that one
request combines, at a single metering boundary: progressive value delivery; usage that is generated at
runtime and therefore unknown at authorization time; multi-category token vectors; tokenizer- and provider-
dependent usage semantics; pricing functions deﬁned over those categories; and asynchronous settlement.
It is the combination we systematize, not the individual pieces.
The three dimensions generalize to diﬀerent degrees, stated rather than blurred. B0 is fully generic: a
shared-counter race that behaves the same in any metered service, and the revised condition of Section 10.4 –
that authorization be atomically coupled to economic commitment on the authorizing path – is a statement
about distributed accounting state with no reference to inference at all. M1 generalizes to any service that
delivers value progressively over a long-running request, streamed media and batch jobs included, and is
interesting exactly where a terminal path can bypass the accounting event. M2 is the narrowest: it needs
a billed quantity that is runtime-produced, multi-category, and priced by a category-sensitive function,
currently most visible in LLM APIs but not unique to them.
The boundary studied here is the usage-metering and accounting subsystem of a network-accessible
serving system: an authorization path, a settlement path, and an accounting event lifecycle, with state
ownership divided among the gateway, the accounting store, and, in the asynchronous family, an event
pipeline, and a data-authority decision at ACCOUNTED about which representation of usage the billing
function reads. We do not study the behavior, robustness, or output safety of the language model itself.

nonparametric tests and bootstrap intervals [ 22] for quantities that genuinely vary, in line with common
guidance for the statistical analysis of randomized experiments in software engineering [ 5].

7 FORMAL VERIFICATION
Measurement shows a defect occurs on the schedules our harness produced; it cannot show a defended
architecture is safe on schedules it did not produce. We therefore encode the lifecycle of Section 4 in TLA+
[47] and check it exhaustively with TLC 2.19 [ 84]. In software-veriﬁcation terms, the speciﬁcation is an
executable architectural model against which each conﬁguration’s safety properties are checked; it veriﬁes
the model, not the implementation.
7.1 Model
One speciﬁcation covers all three dimensions, with each dimension as a Boolean constant, so that the safe
and unsafe architectures are the same state machine under diﬀerent constants, exactly how the testbed
implements them behind one interface. The constants are AtomicDebit for B0, Reserves and AbortSafe for
M1, and ServerAuthority for M2. A ﬁfth constant, ClientMayAbort, is a workload switch rather than an
architecture property; disabling it in the B0 conﬁgurations keeps the synchronization counterexample free
of commitment-timing noise, mirroring how the experiments hold the other dimensions at their safe setting.
Four invariants are checked, each the formal counterpart of a gate the summarizers already apply to every
record: AccountingIntegrity (𝑉 (𝑟 ) ≤ 𝑁 (𝑟 ) for terminal 𝑟 ), Solvency ( balance ≥ 0), LedgerConservation (once
all requests are terminal, 𝐵init − 𝐵final = ∑
𝑟 𝑁 (𝑟 )), and RefundBounded, which is Equation ( 2). Solvency is
checked separately from accounting integrity; collapsing them would destroy the distinction the ablation
exists to establish.
7.2 Method
Each invariant is checked in its own TLC run. TLC halts at the ﬁrst violation it ﬁnds, so checking jointly
would hide the fact that an M2 architecture breaks two of them. The result is a full conﬁguration ×
invariant matrix (Table 4): ten conﬁgurations and four invariants give 40 independent runs over 27,526
distinct states. Every expected outcome is declared in the checking script before the run, which exits non-
zero on any disagreement; all 40 matched. Runs use a single worker deliberately: with parallel workers, a
run that halts at a counterexample explores a nondeterministic number of states, and the totals drifted
between runs.

7.3 Counterexamples
TLC returns a shortest counterexample for each unsafe conﬁguration, and each reproduces the mechanism
we measured. For B0, the 21-step trace is the lost update exactly: both requests read the same balance,
both write an absolute value derived from that stale read, and both then deliver and are charged. Which
invariant fails is the interesting part. Accounting integrity and the refund bound both hold, because every
individual record is internally consistent; what breaks is conservation of the shared balance. That is why a
per-record audit cannot detect B0 while a conservation check can.
For M1, the model checks all four combinations of reservation and abort-safe ﬁnalization, not just the two
the implementation ships. Reservation alone yields solvency and violates integrity. Abort-safe ﬁnalization
alone yields integrity and drives the balance to −1. Only the conjunction satisﬁes both. The orthogonality
claim is therefore established by exhaustive exploration rather than by the two architectures we happened
to build.
For M2, the client-authoritative architecture violates accounting integrity and also the refund bound:
with a reservation in place, under-billing surfaces as an over-refund. That the same defect appears in two
independent invariants is a consequence of the model, not something we set out to show, visible only because
the invariants are checked separately. Enabling server authority restores all four invariants against the same
lying client; the declared value simply stops being read.
The three defenses also compose. With contention, aborts, and a lying client all enabled, no invariant is
violated on any reachable state, at two and at three concurrent requests.
7.4 Evidence labels and their limits
We use three labels and do not interchange them. Model-checked means TLC explored the reachable state
space exhaustively under a stated conﬁguration. Formally argued means stated over the accounting model
and reasoned about by hand. Empirically veriﬁed means measured and independently recomputed. Nothing
weaker than the ﬁrst is ever described as proved.
Nothing here shows a production implementation is safe: the model abstracts away the database, net-
work, partial failure, asynchronous reconciliation, multi-category pricing, and tokenizer disagreement, and
is ﬁnite, at two to three requests with unit pricing; only safety properties are checked, not liveness. We
do not provide a mechanized reﬁnement proof from the abstract speciﬁcation to the implementation; the
correspondence between the two is an argument supported by case-by-case agreement between formal and
empirical outcomes, not more.
7.5 Validating the specification
Declaring expectations before running earned its keep immediately. Our ﬁrst speciﬁcation treated a reser-
vation as a hold and set the committed debit equal to the charge at settlement, so 𝑁 = 𝐷 − 𝐹 fell below
the delivered value and TLC rejected the safe conﬁgurations. The model was wrong, not the paper: in a
reserve-and-reconcile architecture the reservation is the committed debit, and the refund returns its unused
part. Correcting the settlement semantics made the safe conﬁgurations verify; we record the failure because
a formal model edited until it agrees with the paper is worth nothing.

7.6 A second model: the asynchronous-settlement race
The speciﬁcation above assumes strongly consistent accounting state and is silent on the mechanism behind
Section 10.4: deferred settlement, not concurrency, separating authorization from commitment. We built a
second, separate TLA+ speciﬁcation, AsyncSettlementTOCTOU.tla, rather than extending the ﬁrst, because
the two mechanisms are diﬀerent in kind. It is a causal-ordering model, not a timed one: a request moves
through created → admitted/rejected → settlement_pending → settled (or released), and interleavings are
ordered by happens-before, not by milliseconds, matching the T1–T7 instrumentation’s own ﬁnding that
the authorization step is a plain read and only settlement ever writes the balance.
We checked it with TLC at six preregistered (𝑁 , 𝑘) points: (3, 1), (4, 1), (4, 2), (5, 2), (6, 2), and (6, 3).
The (6, 2) point matches the primary B0-async workload, and (4, 2) matches a second tested empirical
conﬁguration; the remaining four are a preregistered bounded extension. In the vulnerable conﬁguration,
Solvency is violated at all six points; TLC’s shortest counterexample at (6, 2) needs only three staggered,
non-simultaneous admissions against a two-unit budget. AccountingIntegrity and LedgerConservation hold
at all six bounded points (the (6, 2) holding runs re-checked in isolation explored 91,921 generated and
22,521 distinct states, with no violation). In the atomic conﬁguration all three invariants hold at all six
points. Across 36 single-invariant runs, all outcomes matched the preregistered expectations; sums across
repeated invariant runs were 480,565 generated and 116,920 distinct states.
This contrasts usefully with Table 4 rather than repeating it: the synchronous model’s vulnerable B0
conﬁguration violates LedgerConservation and holds Solvency, the signature of a lost update; this model’s
vulnerable conﬁguration violates Solvency and holds LedgerConservation, the signature of an admission
race with no lost write at all. Two mechanistically distinct races leave diﬀerent invariant ﬁngerprints, itself
a cross-check between the two speciﬁcations rather than noise to explain away.
The same evidence labels and limits apply as in Section 7’s own account of itself: this is model-checked,
exhaustively, at six ﬁnite (𝑁 , 𝑘) points, over an abstract, untimed model of the mechanism. It does not verify
the implementation, a timed or networked behavior, or anything beyond the six conﬁgurations checked, and
it is the formal counterpart of the empirical result in Section 10.5, not a substitute for it.

8.5 Limits of detectability measurement
A natural extension of this measurement is whether a leak is detectable after the fact from retained evidence,
distinguishing an invisible leak from one that is merely unreconciled. We tested this directly and found that
our instrumentation cannot support the distinction.
Two defects were involved. The implementation assigned the detectability level from static architecture
metadata rather than measuring it, so the experiment read back a label we had set. When we replaced
that with a classiﬁer reading only retained ledger evidence and blind to architecture labels, the distinction
disappeared: the two architectures in question retain identical evidence, so both are equally reconcilable.
Across the full dataset, 2,200 records carrying the stored “invisible” label are reconcilable or prevented on
the evidence, and no record in the study qualiﬁes as invisible at all.
The root cause is a property of the testbed rather than of the architectures: our harness records ground-
truth usage for every architecture because it needs that value to compute leakage, whereas a production
deployment need not retain it. Our instrumentation therefore cannot distinguish detectability classes, so
we make no detectability claim. We report this as a limitation rather than build additional instrumentation
to rescue the metric.

10.3 Asynchronous accounting
Both backends so far settle before the response completes. Real metering pipelines often do not: the gateway
emits a usage event and a separate worker applies it later. We therefore added a third accounting family,
an event queue with a continuously running worker and a controlled reconciliation delay 𝐷, because a claim
of architectural generality that quietly assumes strong consistency is not worth much.
With an honest pipeline, asynchronous accounting is delayed rather than lossy. The mean exposure
window between value delivery and durable debit tracks 𝐷 closely: at 𝐷 = 0, 10, 50, 100, 500 ms the measured
windows are 14.0, 14.9, 54.3, 103.7, and 504.1 ms, and the leak reconciles to exactly zero. Duplicated events
are suppressed idempotently across all 25 injected duplicates. A lost event, by contrast, leaks permanently
at every delay. This is an injected application-level fault (the event is never created), distinct from the
infrastructure-level queue failure discussed in Section 10.6, and the result above is unaﬀected by that
section’s ﬁx. Whether a straggler looks lost or merely late is a property of the observation horizon rather
than of the architecture; we used four times the conﬁgured delay, ﬂoored at 400 ms, and the released artifact
records the per-cell outcome. The reconciliation delays used here are injected experimental parameters, not
delays observed in a production pipeline.
10.4 B0 revisited: the condition is not fundamentally about concurrency
The asynchronous family changed one of our own conclusions, and it is the sharpest systems result in the
paper.
We issued requests strictly sequentially, 20 ms apart, so that no two were ever in ﬂight together. We use
“sequential” throughout this paper in exactly this sense: arrivals do not overlap, but settlement may still be
pending when the next request is authorized. Section 10.5 introduces a deliberately diﬀerent control, strict
serialization of authorization and settlement, which additionally waits for settlement conﬁrmation and is not
interchangeable with the sequential arrivals studied here. Any over-serving in this experiment therefore does
not arise from overlapping in-ﬂight requests. Over-serving nonetheless appears once 𝐷 reaches 100 ms, and
reaches four requests over a two-request budget at 𝐷 = 500 ms, with the balance driven negative (Table 7).
The mechanism is simple once seen. Authorization reads a committed balance that is correct but stale,
because the debits for earlier requests are still in the queue. An atomic guarded decrement, the entire B0
defense, provides nothing if it lands after the next authorization has already read the balance. Concurrency
was never the essential ingredient; it was one way of separating the check from the commitment. Delayed
settlement is another, and it needs no concurrency at all.

Every topology in Section 10 so far ran on a single host over loopback, which leaves open whether Sec-

While building this topology we found that our own sequential-arrival harness had a pacing confound:
it paced requests by sleeping a nominal interval after each full round trip, and over the real network the
round trip itself, not the sleep, dominated the achieved inter-arrival gap, measured directly from request
timestamps at 513–857 ms rather than the intended 20 ms. We ﬁxed the harness to record the achieved
gap per request and reran with a delay range chosen to straddle that measured gap. The corrected sweep

The B0 mechanism persisted under a network-separated two-machine deployment, while strict serializa-
tion of authorization and settlement produced no observed over-serving across the tested settlement delays.
We distinguish the two deliberately: sequential arrivals with deferred settlement (the mechanism above,
and in Section 10.4) means requests do not overlap but settlement may still be pending when the next
request is authorized; strict serialization of authorization and settlement means the harness conﬁrms, from
the account’s own authoritative balance, that settlement for request 𝑖 has completed before request 𝑖 + 1
is dispatched. The strict-serial control polls the authoritative balance until it equals the expected post-
settlement value; an earlier implementation polled the queue’s reported depth instead and was replaced
after we found a transient window in which the queue reports empty while an event is still in transit. Over
the same two-machine topology, strict serialization produced zero over-serving in every one of twelve tested
cells ( 𝐷 ∈ { 0, 500, 2000, 10000} ms, 3 repetitions each; served equaled the budget exactly in all twelve).
This is evidence for one tested topology, one cloud region, and one host pair, not a characterization of
networked deployments in general; Section 13 states the remaining scope precisely.

10.6 Durability of the queued settlement event
Fault injection during the network-separated work surfaced a second, independent ﬁnding about the
testbed’s own queue, distinct from the B0 admission race above. Before this ﬁx, the only record of a
pending settlement event was the message in the Redis queue; no durable record existed until the worker
applied the debit. We tested this directly, not by simulation: we queued an event with its delay not yet
elapsed, conﬁrmed it was queued, hard -terminated the Redis process, restarted it, and found the event
permanently lost, with the queue empty and the corresponding debit never applied.
The ﬁx is a PostgreSQL transactional outbox, described as an implementation pattern in Table 6: the
settlement event is written as a durable row in the same database transaction as the accounting record,
committed before the event is handed to the queue, so a durable record exists independent of the queue
from the moment the request is admitted. A settlement worker claims and applies an event in one atomic
transaction, so no intermediate state exists in which an event is marked settled without the debit having
landed. A queue-independent reconciliation pass scans for due, unsettled events and applies them directly
from the durable record, recovering anything the queue failed to deliver.
We re-ran the targeted failure points this ﬁx addresses and conﬁrmed each by raw evidence, not inference:
a worker terminated before an event was due recovered correctly on restart with exactly one debit applied;
a hard termination of the queue process after an event was queued but not yet due was recovered by the
reconciliation pass once the event’s delay elapsed, rather than lost; duplicate delivery was suppressed with
zero double-charging; and ledger conservation and ﬁnal-balance correctness held in every case tested. We
did not force a live database transaction rollback (argued analytically from the single-transaction design
instead) and did not test a virtual-machine or host restart. Delivery remains at-least-once, not exactly-once:
the queue or the reconciliation pass may both attempt to apply the same event, and the atomic claim
ensures duplicate delivery does not result in a duplicate debit, which is a narrower and veriﬁable guarantee
than claiming no event is ever delivered twice.
We characterize this architecture precisely rather than with a single adjective: before the ﬁx it was process-
crash vulnerable to termination of the queue process (a permanent loss, not merely a recoverable delay);
after the ﬁx it is eventually durable with at-least-once delivery and idempotent settlement application,
at the speciﬁc failure points tested, and not validated against a host restart, arbitrary-scale distributed
failure, or any topology beyond Section 10.5’s own two-machine pair. The ﬁx changes nothing about the
B0 admission race studied throughout this paper – the race is in the authorization read, which it does not
touch – and every pre-existing asynchronous-family experiment (Sections 10.3, 10.4) reproduced identical
results when re-run against it, since none inject a queue failure.
11 ECONOMIC AND PRICING ANALYSIS

a construction of ours.

assuming an attacker population and request volume, and the result would be invention dressed as mea-
surement. What the analysis supports is a design rule: the pricing basis determines the attack surface and
should be chosen with that in mind, not for billing convenience alone.
12 DISCUSSION
Three dimensions of one accounting boundary behave diﬀerently because each is defective in a diﬀerent
element of the lifecycle: B0 is a shared-state property that fails whenever the check is separated from the
commitment, while M1 and M2 are per-request properties with no concurrency dependence in the tested
design (Sections 5 and 8).
The practical consequences are three: an operator cannot use traﬃc level as a proxy for exposure, since
two of the three dimensions do not scale with load at all and the third fails under sequential arrivals if
settlement lags; integrity and solvency need diﬀerent controls, so a system with only one will look correct
in whichever dimension it is measured; and in the evaluated architectures a correct recount did not prevent

Engineering implications. Within the tested and modeled conditions, the results place each control at

the recount that feeds billing must be authoritative rather than merely computed. Every terminal path
of the request lifecycle must reach a durable accounting event – a cancellation-shielded settlement task
for M1, and the transactional outbox of Section 10.6 for the asynchronous family’s queued event. The
implementation patterns we built are not equivalent to the model-level conditions: satisfying a pattern in
another system does not by itself establish the condition there, which is why Table 6 keeps them apart.
These are implications for engineering metered services under the stated accounting model, demonstrated
on LLM inference; Section 13 states the scope precisely.
Relation to known weakness classes. This is the objection we consider most likely, so we state it ourselves:
the individual mechanisms have precedents in isolation (Sections 3 and 3.5), B0 is a known race presented
as a baseline, not a contribution, and we claim no novelty at the primitive level.
Beyond these known primitives, the paper adds the uniﬁcation stated in the contributions list (Section 1),
plus two things no primitive-level description predicts: the M1 ablation shows, by exhaustive model checking
rather than by example, that solvency and accounting integrity are orthogonal and need diﬀerent primitives,
which “there is a streaming bug” does not predict; and the M2 ablation shows an architecture that recounts
usage correctly can still leak if billing is not authoritative. We do not assert practitioners believed a recount
was suﬃcient; we show experimentally that in the evaluated architectures it was not.
13 THREATS TO V ALIDITY
We organize threats as internal, construct, external, and formal validity [ 63, 82], and add one for the degree
of physical distribution actually exercised.
Internal. A single uvicorn worker in the base conﬁguration makes gateway recount numbers an upper
bound; the host is shared and Docker scheduling is not controlled. Local loopback adds a roughly 45 ms ﬂoor
per request, making the gateway ratios conservative for small inputs. Leakage that depends on delivered
tokens is sensitive to disconnect timing at high concurrency: the within-cell standard deviation of delivered
tokens is exactly zero at concurrency 20 or below and about 0.011 at 50 and 100.
Construct. The B0 debit path is a frozen module predating the pluggable accounting backends, so backend
parity covers M1 and M2 only and B0’s storage independence is untested. Our detectability instrumentation
restates experimenter-assigned labels rather than measuring evidence, so we make no detectability claim.
Formal. Evidence labels, proof scope, and what neither speciﬁcation claims are stated in Section 7 and
apply here. The primary speciﬁcation is ﬁnite (two to three concurrent requests, two value chunks, unit
pricing) and assumes strongly consistent accounting state, so it does not cover the asynchronous family.

generator, but not behavior on commercial providers – the cache-split nondeterminism is a property of

atomicity on the settlement path does not prevent over-serving. Pricing tiers are synthetic. More broadly,
the experiments show the modeled accounting failures are realizable under the speciﬁed architectures and
workloads; they do not show any particular commercial provider implements them, that they are prevalent
across deployed LLM services, or that a speciﬁc provider is vulnerable. Public issue and pull-request evidence
cited here shows only that related behaviors are reported in deployed and open-source infrastructure, not a
claim about any named system’s current state. It does not establish production prevalence. The preregistered
1,200-trial terminal-path fault matrix was not executed because the local Docker daemon was unavailable;
it remains a protocol, not a result.
Distribution. Separation in the topology experiments of Section 10 (execution topologies, accounting
backends) is temporal, not physical: those topologies ran on a single host under Docker Compose over
loopback. The asynchronous settlement family was also run with the accounting store, Redis, and settlement
worker on a second machine reached over the real internet (Section 10.5); the condition persisted there, so it
is not an artifact of loopback communication speciﬁcally. This narrows but does not remove the limitation:
we tested one cloud-region host pair, not network partition, clock skew, or cross-machine queueing behavior
in general, and we did not test a VM or host restart, a wide-area or multi-region topology, or a production
deployment. No independent second-host replication was executed in this upgrade: the discussion above
concerns the preserved author-run dataset, whose raw records and manifests were inspected, not a fresh
external validation.
14 RESPONSIBLE DISCLOSURE AND ETHICS
This work produced no new vulnerability ﬁndings against any third-party, live, or production system: all
experiments ran against the local testbed shipped with the released artifact, whose harness defaults to
localhost and contains no third-party endpoints, and no credentials, real user data, or production billing
APIs were used. The public artifacts cited as prior evidence in Section 3.5 were public, and in two cases
already ﬁxed, before this work; they are reports by those projects’ own maintainers and users, not ﬁndings
of ours, and we reviewed only their published issue and pull-request pages. Every attack in the artifact is
paired with a defense, and the testbed is released so operators can measure their own gateways.
