WHAT THE PAPER IS ABOUT

A metered AI-inference service decides whether to admit work before it knows how much
work will be done, delivers the work incrementally, and commits the charge afterwards.
Between admission and commitment the service holds economic state that is authoritative
for billing but not yet durable, and that state is read and written by several
components: an authorization path, an execution path, and a settlement path that may run
asynchronously behind both. This places the usage-metering and billing-settlement
boundary inside the security perimeter of the AI-serving system itself, not outside it:
the question the paper asks is whether such a system can enforce the resource and
economic constraints it claims to enforce on a client it has already authenticated, and
what architectural conditions prevent it from failing to.

LLM inference serving is the workload we use to answer it, because it exhibits every
ingredient at once: progressive value delivery, quantity unknown at authorization time, a
runtime-produced multi-category usage vector, and asynchronous settlement. The paper does
not study the behaviour, robustness, or output safety of the language model itself; it
studies the integrity of the accounting and settlement infrastructure around it.

WHY TAISAP

The contribution is a systematic security analysis of deployed AI-serving
infrastructure, not a new model-level attack.

- Distributed accounting state as a security boundary. A metered request is modelled as
  a lifecycle with one integrity property, V(r) <= N(r), where V is value delivered and N
  is the net committed debit. Three failure mechanisms are localized to three distinct
  elements of that lifecycle: a shared-state transition (B0), a terminal path (M1), and a
  data-authority boundary (M2). B0 is a lost update in the sense of the classical
  isolation-level literature; the paper connects it to that literature rather than
  presenting it as new.

- The central systems result concerns settlement, not concurrency. We issue requests
  strictly sequentially, 20 ms apart, so that no two are ever in flight together, and
  over-serving still appears once the settlement delay exceeds the inter-arrival time.
  This forces a strictly more general condition than the one we began with: authorization
  must be atomically coupled to economic commitment on the path that authorizes service.
  Concurrency is one way to break that coupling; deferred settlement is another.

- Verification. The lifecycle is specified in TLA+ and checked exhaustively with TLC
  across ten configurations and four invariants, with every expected outcome declared in
  the checking script before the runs; all forty matched, over 27,526 distinct states. A
  second, causal-ordering specification covers the asynchronous-settlement mechanism
  separately. Both models are finite, check safety only, and abstract away the database
  and the network. There is no mechanized refinement proof from specification to
  implementation, and the manuscript says so in those words.

- Empirical validation across deployment variation. The same architectures are measured
  across three execution topologies, three accounting families including an asynchronous
  event pipeline, and two independent inference data planes, one of which is third-party
  serving code (llama.cpp serving SmolLM2-135M-Instruct over real server-sent-event
  streaming). Integrity gates fail closed and are validated by fault injection, and a
  separate audit re-derives every reported quantity from raw data using code that imports
  nothing from the project.

CONTRIBUTION, STATED CONSERVATIVELY

We claim no novelty at the primitive level, and say so in the abstract, the introduction,
and the discussion. Relying on a client-supplied value in a security decision is CWE-807;
racing a shared counter is a classical web race; disconnect-related accounting gaps are
documented in deployed gateways. The contribution is the unified accounting model, the
architectural taxonomy, the machine-checked and empirical evidence, and the sufficiency
conditions, not a new attack or a new defense primitive.

PUBLIC EVIDENCE, AND WHAT WE DID NOT DO

Section 3.5 tabulates five public reports from independently developed metering gateways
in which the same architectural ingredient appears, including one in which the project's
own maintainers measured 254,347 completion tokens delivered and never billed in a single
interrupted stream. We reviewed only the published issue and pull-request pages. We
attempted nothing against any third-party system, we make no claim that any listed system
is exploitable, and two of the five reports are already fixed by their own maintainers.
Every measurement in the paper comes from our own local testbed.

ON LIMITATIONS

The formal model is finite and checks safety only; there is no mechanized refinement
proof; no commercial provider was tested, by deliberate ethical constraint; and
real-stack validation uses one external serving stack and one small model on CPU.

One limitation deserves the editor's attention directly, because it bears on the paper's
central systems claim. Separation between authorization and settlement is temporal in
most of our experiments, not physical: the core topologies ran on a single host under
Docker Compose over loopback, with the reconciliation delay an injected parameter under
our control. We additionally repeated the asynchronous-settlement experiment on a
network-separated two-machine deployment reached over the real internet, and the
condition persisted there -- narrowing, though not removing, this limitation. The
manuscript states exactly what was and was not tested rather than leaving a reader to
infer it.

Our detectability analysis is explicitly limited by the instrumentation used:
experimenter-assigned labels were restated rather than independently measured from
retained evidence, so we make no detectability claim.

ARTIFACT AND ETHICS

The testbed, attack harness, defense implementations, both TLA+ specifications, and
complete raw data are publicly released and archived with a DOI. Every table and figure
regenerates from raw data; none is hand-edited. Every attack in the artifact is paired
with a defense. All experiments ran against a local testbed whose harness defaults to
localhost and contains no third-party endpoints. No credentials, real user data, or
production billing APIs were used, and this work produced no vulnerability findings
against any third-party or production system.
