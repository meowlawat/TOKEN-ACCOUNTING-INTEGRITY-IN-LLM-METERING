\title{Token-Accounting Integrity:\\
Characterizing Client-Side Under-Payment in LLM Metering Architectures}
\author{(anonymized for review)}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Usage-based billing is the default commercial model for LLM inference, yet the
\emph{accounting} pipeline that turns delivered tokens into money is itself a
security boundary. Existing work on LLM billing studies two directions: a dishonest
\emph{provider} over-charging an honest client, and a third party inflating a
\emph{victim's} bill. We study the remaining direction --- an honest provider and a
dishonest \emph{client} that seeks inference value while under-paying --- and argue
it is best understood not as a list of attacks but as a single accounting boundary
spanning three architectural dimensions: \textbf{state synchronization} (is the
authorize-and-decrement transition atomic?), \textbf{commitment timing} (is the
charge irrevocable before value is delivered?), and \textbf{usage authority} (who
determines the billed quantity?). We do not claim new attack primitives; the
enabling mechanisms are known individually. Our contribution is to model these
dimensions as transitions of a request-lifecycle state machine, build a reproducible
vulnerable-by-construction testbed, and measure them under integrity gates that
independently re-derive every reported number. Across 2{,}000+ instrumented
requests we find that per-request leakage from commitment-timing and usage-authority
defects is \emph{invariant} to concurrency, whereas the synchronization defect is
\emph{created} by it --- a structural distinction confirmed by an architectural
ablation showing that different primitives close different properties: abort-inclusive
finalization closes the integrity violation while reservation independently provides
the solvency guarantee, and performing a server-side recount is insufficient unless it
is also used as the billing basis. A local real-model experiment bounds the cost of the
strongest defense at $\approx$0.015\% of end-to-end inference time, contradicting the
apparent expense of the same defense measured against a zero-cost mock generator.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Because the billed unit --- the token --- is produced by opaque, streamed, and
long-running inference, the pipeline that authorizes, meters, and commits a charge is
security-relevant. Recent work studies dishonest \emph{providers} inflating that
accounting against honest users~\cite{coin,invisible,tokeninflation}, and
availability/economic attacks that drain a victim's budget~\cite{dow,llmjacking}.
Both assume the client is honest or impersonated. We study the missing quadrant
(Table~\ref{tab:quadrant}): a legitimate but dishonest client that under-pays.

We are deliberately adversarial about our own novelty. Each enabling mechanism is
already known in isolation: a deployed LLM gateway already refunds pre-consumed quota
when a final usage record is missing after a client
disconnect~\cite{newapi5235}; ``never trust client input in a security decision'' is
CWE-807, and a metered-token proxy already mitigates an encoding-induced ``silent
zero-debit''~\cite{aperture247}; and concurrent quota-decrement races are a classic
web-race primitive with a recent CVE~\cite{kettle,cve2026}. Our contribution is
therefore \emph{systematization and measurement}, not discovery: we show these are
three placements of one accounting boundary, localize each to a state transition, and
quantify the integrity/performance trade-off of the defenses.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item An \textbf{architectural taxonomy and accounting model} (\S\ref{sec:statemodel})
that localizes synchronization (B0), commitment timing (M1), and usage authority (M2)
to specific transitions of a request-lifecycle state machine, with a single integrity
property $V(r)\le N(r)$.
\item A \textbf{reproducible client-side under-payment benchmark}: a
vulnerable-by-construction gateway, attack harness, and defense implementations, with
integrity gates that re-derive every reported number from a per-request ledger and
fail the build on any discrepancy.
\item An \textbf{empirical characterization} of B0/M1/M2 across architectures,
concurrency, token volume, abort position, price tier, tokenizer, and workload,
including the negative result that M1/M2 leakage is concurrency-invariant.
\item An \textbf{architectural ablation} identifying which enforcement primitive
closes which property --- a stronger claim than ``the defense works''.
\item An \textbf{integrity/performance trade-off} for authoritative recount, measured
at three levels: standalone tokenizer, gateway end-to-end, and a local real-model
experiment.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Research questions.}
\textbf{RQ1} How does the placement of synchronization, commitment, and usage
authority affect client-side under-payment? \textbf{RQ2} How much inference value can
a dishonest client obtain without authoritative billing? \textbf{RQ3} Which failures
are visible to the application's own logs and reconciliation? \textbf{RQ4} Which
enforcement primitives close each violation, and at what cost?

\section{Threat Model}
Honest provider; dishonest client with valid credentials. Concretely, the attacker
controls only: request payloads and any client-declared usage fields; permitted
headers; request concurrency; the client connection lifecycle (it may abort at any
moment); retries; and observation of its own responses and balance. It \emph{cannot}
compromise TLS, access the database or Redis, read the gateway filesystem, compromise
the inference backend, forge authenticated upstream/provider metadata, or alter server
code. Every listed capability is exercised by the harness; none is granted that we do
not use. This is an application-layer adversary, not a symbolic cryptographic one.

Success on request $r$ means receiving value $V(r)$ while the account is net-charged
less. All experiments run against a local testbed; no third-party system is contacted.

\section{Testbed and Methodology}
FastAPI gateway, PostgreSQL~16 (\textsc{read committed}), Redis~7, a deterministic
mock generator for reproducibility, and real tokenizers for the recount path; Docker
Compose on one commodity host (16 logical CPUs, 16\,GB). Money is exact decimal
(\texttt{NUMERIC(18,6)}). Category-wise pricing over \{input, output, cached,
reasoning\} in three tiers; leakage \emph{efficiency} (fraction of true value evaded)
is the price-invariant headline metric, and absolute dollars are labelled scenario
values.

\paragraph{Integrity gates.} Every summarizer independently recomputes each record's
leak from primitives, checks the invariant flag for consistency, and verifies ledger
conservation $\sum_r N(r) = B_{\text{init}}-B_{\text{final}}$ \emph{without} trusting
the application's own bookkeeping. A violation fails the build. We validate the gates
adversarially by fault injection: corrupting a leak value, a debit, a served flag, a
balance, an invariant flag, or deleting a ledger row must each be detected
(12/12 detected), alongside metamorphic properties (10/10; see
\texttt{results/processed/metamorphic\_report.json}).

\begin{sloppypar}
\paragraph{Statistics.} All reported statistics are produced by the standalone script
\texttt{statistical\_analysis.py} (scipy/statsmodels), which writes
\texttt{stats\_output.txt}; the paper cites that file. Many conditions
here are deterministic by construction (identical input, identical code path), giving
exactly zero variance; for those we report the constant and complete separation rather
than a p-value, and reserve nonparametric tests and bootstrap intervals for quantities
that genuinely vary (latency, throughput, real-model timing).
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Results}

\subsection{B0 --- synchronization (known baseline)}
The non-atomic check-then-decrement leaks only when concurrency $\ge 2$: mean
\$-leak is $0$ at $c{=}1$ and grows exactly linearly, slope $0.099$ per additional
concurrent request with $R^2=1.0000$ ($p=1.9\times10^{-50}$). The hardened atomic
compare-and-decrement leaks \$0 at every level (Table~\ref{tab:b0},
Fig.~\ref{fig:b0}).

\subsection{M1 --- commitment timing}
Leakage tracks the value delivered before the (missing) commit: it rises monotonically
with abort position and collapses to zero at completion, when the debit finally fires
(Fig.~\ref{fig:m1}, Table~\ref{tab:m1}). Both vulnerable architectures --- ``debit only
after completion'' and ``refund the whole reservation on abort'' --- are economically
identical.

\paragraph{Concurrency is not the mechanism.} Holding request volume constant, leak per
request is invariant across $c\in\{1,5,20,50,100\}$ to four decimal places, and
request-ASR is exactly $1.0$ (vulnerable) or $0.0$ (safe) at every level
(Table~\ref{tab:m1conc}, Fig.~\ref{fig:m1conc}). The honest-client control arm leaks
zero everywhere. M1 is a per-request accounting-design defect, not a race.

\subsection{M2 --- usage authority}
Client-authoritative billing leaks for most manipulations, and \emph{which}
manipulation succeeds depends on the billing basis: under category pricing a
total/subtotal mismatch is inert, while under flat-total pricing it becomes the
strongest attack (Table~\ref{tab:m2}, Fig.~\ref{fig:m2}). Every server-authoritative
architecture drives leakage efficiency to $0.000$ across all manipulations, and is
likewise concurrency-invariant (Table~\ref{tab:m2conc}).

\subsection{Architectural ablation: which primitive closes what?}
Removing one primitive at a time (Tables~\ref{tab:m1abl},~\ref{tab:m2abl}) yields the
paper's sharpest architectural result:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{M1:} \emph{abort-inclusive finalization} closes the integrity violation
--- with or without a reservation. \emph{Reservation} closes a \emph{different}
property: without it the balance goes negative under a constrained budget (solvency),
even though integrity holds. Neither primitive substitutes for the other.
\item \textbf{M2:} performing a recount is \emph{not} sufficient. The
\texttt{client\_logged} architecture computes an authoritative recount and still leaks
at efficiency $0.595$, because billing still uses the client's number. The recount must
be \emph{used as the billing basis}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Lifecycle failure modes}
Beyond the scripted attack, we inject nine lifecycle failures (disconnect at $t{=}0$
and mid-stream, truncated stream, settle-window race, missing / delayed / duplicated
usage metadata, and a recount-engine failure). Every invariant violation falls on a
vulnerable architecture; the hardened architectures survive all of them, and a
recount-engine failure is \emph{fail-closed} --- the request is rejected and nothing is
served or billed (Table~\ref{tab:failmodes}).

\subsection{Detectability}
Detection is a second defense axis (Table~\ref{tab:detect}). Under
\texttt{client}-authoritative billing the leak is invisible (D0): the ledger faithfully
records the client's lie. Merely \emph{logging} a server recount moves the same leak to
D1 --- recoverable by a post-hoc reconciliation pass --- at negligible cost, and
reconciling in-request reaches D3. This gives operators who cannot re-architect billing
an immediate, cheap intermediate step.

\subsection{Cost of authoritative recount (RQ4)}
We measure the strongest defense at three levels, because they answer different
questions.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Standalone tokenizer} (Fig.~\ref{fig:toklat}): cost is linear in input
length (log--log slope $0.999$--$1.14$, $R^2\ge0.998$), so it budgets as
tokens\,$\times$\,a per-token constant. Engine choice matters by $2.9$--$3.6\times$ at
16{,}384 tokens; at a fixed token count, workload changes cost by up to
$3.2\times$.
\item \textbf{Gateway end-to-end with a \emph{mock} generator}: recount is nearly free
at $\le$1{,}024 tokens ($\ge0.96\times$ throughput) but costs heavily at 16{,}384
tokens ($0.05$--$0.76\times$). Tokenization time explains this
(Spearman $\rho=-0.868$, $p=1.3\times10^{-5}$).
\item \textbf{Local real-model experiment} (external validity): with \emph{real}
autoregressive generation, the recount is
$\mathbf{0.012\%}$--$\mathbf{0.016\%}$ of end-to-end time at every context size
(bootstrap 95\% CIs in \texttt{stats\_output.txt}); measured throughput ratios scatter
around $1.0$ ($0.89$--$1.05$) with signs that flip across workloads, i.e.\ within
timing noise.
\end{enumerate}
The apparent expense in (2) is an artifact of the mock generator costing
\emph{nothing}: when generation is free, tokenization is the whole request. Under real
inference the same defense is negligible. We report all three rather than the most
convenient one.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
The mechanisms are reframings of known issues, not new primitives; we state this
throughout and use bounded language for the literature gap (``we found no prior work in
our targeted corpus'', never ``nobody has studied this''). Principal threats: the
generator is a mock in all but the external-validity experiment, and that experiment
uses a small model on CPU --- it bounds a \emph{ratio}, and says nothing about any
commercial provider; the gateway runs a single uvicorn worker, so its recount numbers
are an upper bound; measurements are single-node over loopback, and high-concurrency
gateway ratios are noisy (repeat spread up to 17\%), so only low-concurrency columns are
treated as defensible; and the price tiers are synthetic, which is why leakage efficiency
rather than dollars is the headline. A full list is in
\texttt{paper/threats\_to\_validity.md}.

\section{Conclusion}
Client-side under-payment in LLM metering is best modelled not as a list of attacks but
as one accounting boundary with three placements: synchronization, commitment timing,
and usage authority. Localizing each to a state transition predicts --- and our
measurements confirm --- that only the synchronization defect is concurrency-created,
and that different primitives close integrity and solvency. The defenses are ordinary;
the contribution is knowing precisely which one is load-bearing, and what it costs.
