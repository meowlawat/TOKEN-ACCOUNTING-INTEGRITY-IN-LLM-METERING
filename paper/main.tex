\title{Token-Accounting Integrity:\\
Measuring Client-Side Under-Payment in LLM Metering Architectures}
\author{(anonymized for review)}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Usage-based billing is the default commercial model for LLM inference, yet the
accounting pipeline that converts delivered tokens into money is itself a security
boundary. Prior work on LLM billing studies a dishonest \emph{provider} over-charging
an honest client, a third party inflating a \emph{victim's} bill, and a dishonest
\emph{intermediary} forging provenance. We study the remaining direction: an honest
provider and a dishonest \emph{client} that seeks inference value while under-paying.
We argue this is not a list of attacks but one accounting boundary with three
placements --- \textbf{state synchronization}, \textbf{economic commitment timing}, and
\textbf{usage authority} --- and we model each as a transition of a request-lifecycle
state machine with a single integrity property, $V(r)\le N(r)$. We do not claim new
attack primitives; the enabling mechanisms are individually known. On a
vulnerable-by-construction testbed with integrity gates that independently re-derive
every reported number, we measure all three across architectures, concurrency, abort
position, price tier, tokenizer, and workload. Leakage from commitment-timing and
usage-authority defects is \emph{invariant} to concurrency (identical to four decimal
places from 1 to 100 concurrent requests), whereas the synchronization defect is
\emph{created} by it (slope $0.099$ per additional concurrent request, $R^2=1.0000$).
An ablation shows different primitives close different properties: abort-safe
finalization closes accounting integrity while reservation independently provides
solvency, and computing a server-side recount is insufficient unless it is also the
billing basis --- an architecture that computes a correct recount and bills the client's
number still leaks $58.3\%$ of the delivered value. A local real-model experiment bounds
the strongest defense's cost at $0.014$--$0.019\%$ of end-to-end inference time,
correcting a $0.05$--$0.76\times$ throughput figure measured against a zero-cost mock
generator. All results regenerate from raw data in a 56-minute clean run.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Because the billed unit --- the token --- is produced by opaque, streamed, long-running
inference, the pipeline that authorizes, meters, and commits a charge is
security-relevant. Recent work studies dishonest \emph{providers} inflating that
accounting~\cite{coin,invisible,tokeninflation}, economic attacks that drain a victim's
budget~\cite{dow,llmjacking}, and dishonest \emph{intermediaries} that misroute or forge
provenance~\cite{aex,gwprov}. All three assume the client is honest, impersonated, or
irrelevant. We study the remaining direction (Table~\ref{tab:quadrant}): a legitimate but
dishonest client that under-pays.

We are deliberately adversarial about our own contribution. Each enabling mechanism is
already known in isolation: a deployed LLM gateway refunds pre-consumed quota when a final
usage record is missing after a client disconnect~\cite{newapi5235}; ``never trust client
input in a security decision'' is CWE-807, and a metered-token proxy already mitigates an
encoding-induced ``silent zero-debit''~\cite{aperture247}; concurrent quota-decrement races
are a classic web-race primitive with a recent CVE~\cite{kettle,cve2026}; and practitioner
guidance already warns that retries and disconnects must not cause token
leakage~\cite{practice}. Accordingly, \textbf{M1 and M2 are systematization and
measurement contributions, and B0 is a known baseline}; we make no priority claim.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{An accounting model} (\S\ref{sec:statemodel}) connecting inference
execution, value delivery, authorization, reservation, debit, reconciliation and refund
in a request-lifecycle state machine, with a formal integrity property that admits
legitimate refunds.
\item \textbf{An architecture taxonomy} of three experimentally distinguishable
dimensions --- synchronization (B0, known baseline), commitment timing (M1), and usage
authority (M2) --- with the known/systematized/defensive boundaries stated explicitly.
\item \textbf{A reproducible measurement study}: a testbed and methodology quantifying
attack success, leakage, leakage efficiency, detectability, invariant violations,
latency and throughput, with integrity gates proven to fail closed and an independent
re-implementation of the strongest defense.
\item \textbf{An integrity/performance trade-off}, including an ablation identifying
which primitive closes which property, and recount cost measured at three levels
(standalone tokenizer, gateway, and a local real-model experiment).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threat Model and Accounting Invariant}
\label{sec:threat}
Honest provider; dishonest client holding valid credentials. The client controls exactly
six things, each exercised by our harness: request payloads including any client-declared
usage fields; permitted headers; request concurrency; the connection lifecycle (it may
abort at any moment); retries; and observation of its own responses and balance. It
\emph{cannot} compromise TLS, access the database or Redis, read the gateway filesystem,
compromise the inference backend, forge authenticated upstream metadata, or alter server
code. This is a concrete application-layer adversary, not a symbolic cryptographic one.
Every leak we report is therefore attributable to the provider's own accounting design
rather than to a compromise.

For a request $r$, let $V(r)$ be the value actually delivered, $D(r)$ the committed
debits, $F(r)$ refunds, and $N(r)=D(r)-F(r)$ the net committed debit. Under-payment and
the integrity property are
\[
\mathrm{Leak}(r)=V(r)-N(r),\qquad \mathrm{Integrity}(r):\;V(r)\le N(r).
\]
The property is an inequality on the \emph{net} debit precisely so that legitimate
reconciliation is admitted: a client that consumes less than a reservation must be
refunded the difference. A refund is legitimate only when it releases the unconsumed
portion, $F(r)\le \mathrm{Res}(r)-V(r)$; the vulnerable ``refund the whole reservation on
abort'' architecture violates exactly this side condition.

\section{Background and Related Work}
LLM APIs meter multi-category token vectors (input, output, cached, reasoning), stream
output incrementally, and settle charges asynchronously. Provider-side auditing work
establishes that hidden-token accounting can be inflated and is hard to
verify~\cite{coin,invisible,tokeninflation}; Denial-of-Wallet and LLMjacking establish
victim-side economic exposure~\cite{dow,llmjacking}; attestation work establishes
intermediary-side provenance~\cite{aex,gwprov}. Generic quota races are well
understood~\cite{kettle,cve2026}. What we found no prior study of, \emph{in our targeted
corpus}, is an evaluation of client-side under-payment under an honest-provider threat
model across representative metering architectures with a common measurement and an
ablation of the enforcement primitives. This is a bounded negative from a targeted
search; it does not establish non-existence.

\section{Taxonomy and Testbed}
\label{sec:taxonomy}
The three dimensions are experimentally distinguishable, not merely conceptual:
\textbf{B0} (synchronization) is a non-atomic authorize-then-decrement, is
\emph{concurrency-created}, and is labelled a \textbf{known baseline} used to validate
the measurement rig and to provide a generic reference point; \textbf{M1} (commitment
timing) is lifecycle-sensitive; \textbf{M2} (usage authority) is authority- and
pricing-sensitive. Applying the LLM-specificity test explicitly --- \emph{would this
attack work unchanged against a non-LLM API?} --- B0 answers \emph{yes} (hence baseline),
while M1 depends on streamed, long-running delivery and M2 on multi-category token
pricing, so both answer \emph{no}.

The testbed is a FastAPI gateway over PostgreSQL~16 (\textsc{read committed}) and
Redis~7, with a deterministic mock generator for reproducibility and \emph{real}
tokenizers on the recount path, run under Docker Compose on one commodity host (16
logical CPUs, 16\,GB). Money is exact decimal. Five M1 architectures and six M2
architectures share common interfaces so that experiments compare \emph{semantics}
rather than unrelated code.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
Category-wise pricing over three tiers; leakage \emph{efficiency} (fraction of true value
evaded) is the price-invariant headline metric and absolute dollars are labelled scenario
values. Sweeps: B0 concurrency $\{1,2,5,10,20,30,50\}\times30$ repetitions; M1
architecture $\times$ abort position $\times$ concurrency $\{1,5,20,50,100\}$; M2
architecture $\times$ manipulation $\times$ concurrency. Request volume is held
\emph{constant} across concurrency levels so that volume cannot masquerade as
vulnerability.

\paragraph{Integrity gates.} Every summarizer independently recomputes each record's leak
from primitives, checks invariant-flag consistency, and verifies ledger conservation
$\sum_r N(r)=B_{\text{init}}-B_{\text{final}}$ without trusting the application's own
bookkeeping; a violation fails the build. We validate the gates adversarially: corrupting
a leak value, a debit, a served flag, a balance, an invariant flag, or deleting a ledger
row must each be detected (12/12), alongside metamorphic properties (10/10). The
metamorphic suite also caught one of our own unjustified assumptions --- we had asserted
leakage efficiency was price-tier invariant, but that holds only under \emph{uniform}
price scaling, not under a structural change to the price ratios --- which we corrected
rather than suppressed.

\paragraph{Independent verification.} The strongest M2 defense is re-implemented in a
module sharing no code path with the gateway; verdicts agree on all cross-validated cells
(42 M2, 16 M1, 2 B0). The independent checker initially reported three disagreements,
which were \emph{definitional}, not defects: it aggregated under-payment only, while the
gateway aggregates the signed billing delta. Under flat-total pricing an honest client is
systematically \emph{overcharged}, so the two aggregates legitimately differ. We now
report both, and use under-payment for the security property.

\begin{sloppypar}
\paragraph{Statistics.} All statistics are produced by a standalone script
(scipy/statsmodels) into \texttt{stats\_output.txt}, which the paper cites. Many
conditions here are deterministic by construction and have exactly zero variance; for
those we report the constant and complete separation rather than a p-value, and reserve
nonparametric tests and bootstrap intervals for quantities that genuinely vary.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Results}

\subsection{B0 --- synchronization (known baseline)}
The non-atomic check-then-decrement leaks only when concurrency $\ge 2$: mean \$-leak is
$0.000$ at $c{=}1$, then $0.099$, $2.871$ and $4.851$ at $c{=}2$, $30$ and $50$, growing
exactly linearly (slope $0.099$ per additional concurrent request, $R^2=1.0000$,
$p=1.9\times10^{-50}$). The hardened atomic compare-and-decrement leaks \$0 at every
level (Table~\ref{tab:b0}, Fig.~\ref{fig:b0}). B0 is not a contribution; it validates the
rig and provides the contrast used below.

\subsection{M1 --- commitment timing}
Leakage tracks the value delivered before the missing commit, rising monotonically with
abort position and collapsing to zero at completion when the debit finally fires: per
request $0.0045$ at $\sim$1 token delivered, $0.0285$ at 25\%, $0.0540$ at 50\%,
$0.0960$ at 90\%, and $0.0000$ at 100\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:m1}, Table~\ref{tab:m1}).
Request-ASR is $1.00$ at every abort position before completion. The two vulnerable
architectures --- ``debit only after completion'' and ``refund the whole reservation on
abort'' --- are economically identical.

\paragraph{M1 comprises two orthogonal security properties.}
\begin{sloppypar}
\emph{Property A (solvency)}: served value must not exceed the affordable balance.
\emph{Property B (accounting finality)}: delivered value must not exceed the net
committed debit. The ablation separates them (Table~\ref{tab:m1matrix}). With a budget
for two requests and six issued, \texttt{no\_reserve\_settle} preserves accounting
integrity (leak $0.0000$ at every abort position) yet serves all six and drives the
balance to $-0.32$; conversely \texttt{reserve\_refund\_on\_abort} holds a reservation
but refunds it away, failing both. \textbf{Reservation and abort-safe finalization are
orthogonal controls; neither substitutes for the other.}
\end{sloppypar}

\paragraph{Concurrency is not the mechanism.} Holding request volume constant, leak per
request is $0.0528$ at $c=1,5,20,50$ and $100$ --- identical to four decimal places ---
and request-ASR is exactly $1.000$ (vulnerable) or $0.000$ (safe) at every level
(Table~\ref{tab:m1conc}, Fig.~\ref{fig:m1conc}). The honest-client control arm leaks
zero everywhere.

\subsection{M2 --- usage authority}
Writing $U$ for the true usage vector, $P$ for the pricing function and $T$ for the
attacker's transformation of the declared usage, a manipulation pays off exactly when
$P(T(U))<P(U)$ --- that is, when the pricing basis \emph{reads the field $T$ changes}.
Table~\ref{tab:m2formal} derives this per manipulation: a total/subtotal mismatch is inert
under category pricing (which never reads the declared total) and is the strongest attack
under flat-total pricing ($0.741$), while reclassifying output into a discounted cached
category is the reverse. The exploitable set is a property of the billing function, not of
attacker ingenuity. Every server-authoritative architecture drives leakage efficiency to
$0.000$ across all manipulations, and M2 is likewise concurrency-invariant
(Table~\ref{tab:m2conc}).

\paragraph{Usage computation is not usage authority.} The decisive case is
\texttt{client\_logged}: it \emph{computes} a correct server-side recount, logs it, and
still leaks at efficiency $\mathbf{0.583}$, because billing continues to use the client's
number (Table~\ref{tab:m2abl}). A correct recount that is not authoritative for billing
buys detection, not prevention.

\paragraph{Economic sensitivity.} Leakage efficiency is exactly invariant under uniform
price scaling ($\times10$) and shifts by at most $0.012$ under a price-\emph{structure}
change, so the qualitative conclusions are price-independent (Table~\ref{tab:econ}). A
side effect of the same asymmetry is that flat-total pricing systematically
\emph{overcharges} honest clients (efficiency $-0.058$).

\paragraph{Concurrency does not amplify these failures.}
Increased concurrency is not itself a sufficient amplifier of client-side metering
evasion. In the tested architecture, B0 exhibited a concurrency-created race, whereas M1
and M2 remained primarily architectural accounting failures. Reporting this negative
result matters: it means an operator cannot infer safety from low traffic, and cannot
detect M1/M2 by watching for load spikes.

\subsection{Lifecycle failure modes}
Beyond the scripted attack we inject nine lifecycle failures (disconnect at $t{=}0$ and
mid-stream, truncated stream, settle-window race, missing / delayed / duplicated usage
metadata, and a recount-engine failure). Every invariant violation falls on a vulnerable
architecture; the hardened architectures survive all of them; and a recount-engine failure
is \emph{fail-closed} --- the request is rejected and nothing is served or billed
(Table~\ref{tab:failmodes}).

\subsection{Detectability}
Detectability is orthogonal to exploitability: a failure can be exploitable and hidden,
exploitable but reconcilable, or prevented outright. We classify D0 (no evidence), D1
(post-hoc reconciliation only), D2 (logged discrepancy), D3 (prevented in real time).
Under \texttt{client} billing the leak is D0 --- the ledger faithfully records the
client's lie. Merely \emph{logging} a recount moves the same leak to D1 without preventing
it, and reconciling in-request reaches D3 (Table~\ref{tab:detect}). This gives operators
who cannot yet re-architect billing a cheap intermediate step.

\section{Defense Evaluation}
Each vulnerable architecture is paired with a server-side primitive: atomic
compare-and-decrement (B0), reserve-then-abort-safe-finalization (M1), and
server-authoritative recount (M2). Under every defense the invariant-violation rate and
leakage efficiency are zero across the full attack catalogue.

\paragraph{What the strongest defense costs.} We measure recount at three levels because
they answer different questions.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Standalone tokenizer} (Fig.~\ref{fig:toklat}): cost is linear in input
length (log--log slope $0.999$--$1.14$, $R^2\ge0.998$), so it budgets as
tokens\,$\times$\,a per-token constant ($\approx$0.7\,\textmu s/token for tiktoken,
$\approx$2.5\,\textmu s/token for SentencePiece). At 16{,}384 tokens the engines span
$10.1$--$41.8$\,ms, a $2.9$--$3.6\times$ implementation effect; at a fixed token count,
workload changes cost by up to $3.4\times$.
\item \textbf{Gateway with a \emph{mock} generator}: recount is nearly free at
$\le$1{,}024 tokens ($\ge0.96\times$ throughput) but costs heavily at 16{,}384 tokens
($0.05$--$0.76\times$ at $c\!\in\!\{1,10\}$). Tokenization time explains this
(Spearman $\rho=-0.868$, $p=1.3\times10^{-5}$).
\item \textbf{Local real-model experiment}: with real autoregressive generation the
recount is $\mathbf{0.014\%}$--$\mathbf{0.019\%}$ of end-to-end time across 128-, 1024-
and 4096-token contexts, and measured throughput ratios scatter around $1.0$ with signs
that flip across workloads, i.e.\ within a $\pm$10\% noise band that is itself
$\sim$700$\times$ larger than the effect.
\end{enumerate}
The apparent expense in (2) is an artifact of the mock generator costing \emph{nothing}:
when generation is free, tokenization is the entire request. We report all three rather
than the most convenient one, and we do \emph{not} claim recount is ``essentially free'':
it is a real, predictable cost that is small \emph{relative to real inference} in the
tested configuration.

\section{Discussion}
Three placements of one accounting boundary behave differently under load, and the
difference is predicted by \emph{which} element of the state machine is defective:
transition atomicity (B0) is a shared-resource property and therefore concurrency-created,
whereas the transition graph (M1) and data authority (M2) are per-request properties and
therefore concurrency-invariant. The practical consequences are that (i) an operator
cannot use traffic level as a proxy for exposure, (ii) integrity and solvency require
different controls, and (iii) a correct recount is worthless for prevention unless it is
authoritative for billing. The defenses themselves are ordinary; the contribution is
knowing precisely which one is load-bearing and what it costs.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\paragraph{Internal.} Single uvicorn worker, so gateway recount numbers are an upper
bound; shared host and Docker scheduling; local loopback adds a $\sim$45\,ms floor per
request on the Windows/Docker Desktop configuration, which makes the gateway ratios
conservative for small inputs; mock generation everywhere except the real-model
experiment; leakage that depends on delivered tokens is sensitive to disconnect timing at
high concurrency (the within-cell standard deviation of delivered tokens is exactly zero
at $c\le20$ and $\approx$0.011 at $c\in\{50,100\}$).

\paragraph{Gateway measurements at $c\ge50$.} We exclude these from headline claims. The
repeat spread reaches 17\% of the median and the no-recount baseline is itself
non-monotonic, indicating client-side and event-loop saturation rather than server cost;
single runs even produced ratios $>1$, which is physically impossible for added work, and
motivated the switch to median-of-three repeats. Only $c=1$ and $c=10$ are treated as
evidence.

\paragraph{Construct.} Synthetic price tiers; a token-count abstraction of ``inference
value''; leakage normalized by delivered value; and a detectability scale that separates
exploitability from evidence availability.

\paragraph{External.} One local architecture family, one database, one cache, one small
CPU model, no commercial provider, no multi-region or distributed gateway. The real-model
experiment bounds a \emph{ratio} in one configuration and does not validate any
commercial provider's billing behaviour.

\paragraph{Literature.} A targeted search rather than a proof of absence, in a
fast-moving area with terminology mismatch risk.

\section{Responsible Disclosure and Ethics}
This work produced no new vulnerability findings against any third-party, live, or
production system; all experiments ran against the local testbed shipped with the
artifact, whose harness defaults to \texttt{localhost} and contains no third-party
endpoints. No credentials, real user data, or production billing APIs were used. The
public artifacts we cite as prior evidence (a GitHub issue~\cite{newapi5235}, a
PR~\cite{aperture247}, and a CVE~\cite{cve2026}) were public before this work and are not
our findings. Every attack in the artifact is paired with a defense, and the testbed is
released so operators can measure their own gateways.

\section{Conclusion}
Client-side under-payment in LLM metering is best modelled as one accounting boundary
with three placements: synchronization, commitment timing, and usage authority.
Localizing each to a state transition predicts --- and our measurements confirm --- that
only the synchronization defect is concurrency-created, that integrity and solvency are
closed by different primitives, and that a server-side recount must be authoritative
rather than merely computed. In the tested configuration that defense costs a small,
predictable fraction of real inference time.
