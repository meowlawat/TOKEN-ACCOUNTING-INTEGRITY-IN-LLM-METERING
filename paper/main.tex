\title{Token-Accounting Integrity:\\
Measuring Client-Side Under-Payment in LLM Metering Architectures}
\author{(anonymized for review)}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Usage-based billing is a security boundary in LLM inference: value is streamed to the
client before accounting is necessarily finalized, and the charge depends on a
multi-category usage record produced at runtime. We study client-side under-payment
under an honest-provider threat model, and model this accounting-security boundary along
three dimensions --- \textbf{state synchronization}, \textbf{commitment timing}, and
\textbf{usage authority} --- unified by a single integrity property, $V(r)\le N(r)$, over
a request-lifecycle model. \emph{We do not claim new attack primitives}: the enabling
mechanisms are individually known, and the contribution is a systematic measurement and
architectural analysis of them across representative metering designs, together with an
ablation of the enforcement primitives. In controlled experiments the known
synchronization race is \emph{created} by concurrency (slope $0.099$ per additional
concurrent request, $R^2=1.0000$), whereas commitment-timing and usage-authority failures
remain per-request phenomena: under fixed request volume in the tested architecture their
per-request leakage was unchanged across concurrency levels 1--100. For commitment
timing, an ablation shows that abort-safe finalization closes accounting-integrity
violations while reservation independently provides solvency, and that neither
substitutes for the other. For usage authority, computing an accurate server-side recount
is insufficient unless that value becomes the billing basis: an architecture that
recounts correctly, records the result, and still bills the client-declared number leaks
$58.3\%$ of the delivered value. Server-authoritative billing eliminates the observed
usage-authority leakage. To test whether these are artifacts of one execution or storage
design, we repeat the experiments across three topologies (single worker; a reverse proxy
over four workers; a load balancer over two gateway instances) and two accounting
backends (a mutable balance row; an append-only ledger with a derived balance):
$96$ accounting cells match the single-worker control with no mismatches, and $17/17$
strongest cases are identical across backends. We also state model-level sufficiency
conditions per mechanism, separating what is sufficient in the model from what is
implementation-specific. Tokenizer cost is real, grows with input length, and varies by
$2.9$--$3.6\times$ across engines; in the tested local SmolLM2-135M CPU configuration the
recount accounted for $0.014$--$0.019\%$ of end-to-end inference time. All reported
results regenerate from raw data through fail-closed integrity gates and a clean
reproduction pipeline. These results position LLM metering as an accounting-security
boundary whose synchronization, lifecycle, and authority choices determine both
under-payment exposure and defense cost.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Because the billed unit --- the token --- is produced by opaque, streamed, long-running
inference, the pipeline that authorizes, meters, and commits a charge is
security-relevant. Recent work studies dishonest \emph{providers} inflating that
accounting~\cite{coin,invisible,tokeninflation}, economic attacks that drain a victim's
budget~\cite{dow,llmjacking}, and dishonest \emph{intermediaries} that misroute or forge
provenance~\cite{aex,gwprov}. All three assume the client is honest, impersonated, or
irrelevant. We study the remaining direction (Table~\ref{tab:quadrant}): a legitimate but
dishonest client that under-pays.

We are deliberately adversarial about our own contribution. Each enabling mechanism is
already known in isolation: a deployed LLM gateway refunds pre-consumed quota when a final
usage record is missing after a client disconnect~\cite{newapi5235}; ``never trust client
input in a security decision'' is CWE-807, and a metered-token proxy already mitigates an
encoding-induced ``silent zero-debit''~\cite{aperture247}; concurrent quota-decrement races
are a classic web-race primitive with a recent CVE~\cite{kettle,cve2026}; and practitioner
guidance already warns that retries and disconnects must not cause token
leakage~\cite{practice}. Accordingly, \textbf{M1 and M2 are systematization and
measurement contributions, and B0 is a known baseline}; we make no priority claim.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{An accounting model} (\S\ref{sec:statemodel}) connecting inference
execution, value delivery, authorization, reservation, debit, reconciliation and refund
in a request-lifecycle state machine, with a formal integrity property that admits
legitimate refunds.
\item \textbf{An architecture taxonomy} of three experimentally distinguishable
dimensions --- synchronization (B0, known baseline), commitment timing (M1), and usage
authority (M2) --- with the known/systematized/defensive boundaries stated explicitly.
\item \textbf{A reproducible, cross-architecture measurement study}: a testbed and
methodology quantifying attack success, leakage, leakage efficiency, detectability,
invariant violations, latency and throughput --- repeated across three execution
topologies and two accounting backends, with integrity gates proven to fail closed and an
independent re-implementation of the strongest defense.
\item \textbf{Defense sufficiency conditions and their integrity/performance cost}:
model-level sufficient conditions per mechanism (\S\ref{sec:sufficiency}), an ablation
identifying which primitive closes which property, and recount cost measured at three
levels (standalone tokenizer, gateway, and a local real-model experiment).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threat Model and Accounting Invariant}
\label{sec:threat}
Honest provider; dishonest client holding valid credentials. The client controls exactly
six things, each exercised by our harness: request payloads including any client-declared
usage fields; permitted headers; request concurrency; the connection lifecycle (it may
abort at any moment); retries; and observation of its own responses and balance. It
\emph{cannot} compromise TLS, access the database or Redis, read the gateway filesystem,
compromise the inference backend, forge authenticated upstream metadata, or alter server
code. This is a concrete application-layer adversary, not a symbolic cryptographic one.
Every leak we report is therefore attributable to the provider's own accounting design
rather than to a compromise.

For a request $r$, let $V(r)$ be the value actually delivered, $D(r)$ the committed
debits, $F(r)$ refunds, and $N(r)=D(r)-F(r)$ the net committed debit. Under-payment and
the integrity property are
\[
\mathrm{Leak}(r)=V(r)-N(r),\qquad \mathrm{Integrity}(r):\;V(r)\le N(r).
\]
The property is an inequality on the \emph{net} debit precisely so that legitimate
reconciliation is admitted: a client that consumes less than a reservation must be
refunded the difference. A refund is legitimate only when it releases the unconsumed
portion, $F(r)\le \mathrm{Res}(r)-V(r)$; the vulnerable ``refund the whole reservation on
abort'' architecture violates exactly this side condition.

\paragraph{Two distinct properties.} Accounting integrity is not the only thing a
metering system must provide, and conflating the two obscures the defense analysis. We
therefore separate:
\begin{description}\itemsep1pt
\item[Accounting integrity] $V(r)\le N(r)$ --- the client is never net-charged less than
the value it received.
\item[Solvency] a request is served only when the system holds sufficient committed or
reserved economic capacity to cover it under the system's policy.
\end{description}
These are orthogonal. A system can bill exactly for what it delivered while still serving
clients who cannot pay (integrity holds, solvency fails), or hold funds and then release
them (solvency machinery present, integrity fails). In the M1 dimension, \emph{reservation
addresses solvency} and \emph{abort-safe finalization addresses accounting integrity};
\S\ref{sec:m1} shows empirically that neither substitutes for the other
(Table~\ref{tab:m1matrix}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
LLM APIs meter multi-category token vectors (input, output, cached, reasoning), stream
output incrementally, and settle charges asynchronously. Provider-side auditing work
establishes that hidden-token accounting can be inflated and is hard to
verify~\cite{coin,invisible,tokeninflation}; Denial-of-Wallet and LLMjacking establish
victim-side economic exposure~\cite{dow,llmjacking}; attestation work establishes
intermediary-side provenance~\cite{aex,gwprov}. Generic quota races are well
understood~\cite{kettle,cve2026}. What we found no prior study of, \emph{in our targeted
corpus}, is an evaluation of client-side under-payment under an honest-provider threat
model across representative metering architectures with a common measurement and an
ablation of the enforcement primitives. This is a bounded negative from a targeted
search; it does not establish non-existence.

\section{Taxonomy and Testbed}
\label{sec:taxonomy}
The three dimensions are experimentally distinguishable, not merely conceptual:
\textbf{B0} (synchronization) is a non-atomic authorize-then-decrement, is
\emph{concurrency-created}, and is labelled a \textbf{known baseline} used to validate
the measurement rig and to provide a generic reference point; \textbf{M1} (commitment
timing) is lifecycle-sensitive; \textbf{M2} (usage authority) is authority- and
pricing-sensitive.

\paragraph{On LLM specificity.} We do \emph{not} claim these mechanisms are uniquely
possible in LLM systems. Streaming responses, trusting client-supplied values, and racing
a counter all occur elsewhere. What is specific to LLM inference is that a single request
combines, in one metering boundary: progressive (streamed) value delivery;
\emph{runtime-generated} usage that is not known when the request is authorized;
multi-category token vectors (input, output, cached, reasoning); tokenizer- and
provider-dependent usage semantics; pricing functions defined over those usage
categories; and asynchronous reconciliation or settlement. The mechanisms are therefore
not claimed as unique security primitives; rather, LLM inference combines the lifecycle,
usage-authority and token-vector accounting conditions into one boundary, and it is that
combination we systematize and measure. On this basis B0 remains a \emph{generic}
baseline --- a shared-counter race that behaves identically in any metered API --- while
M1 and M2 are LLM-relevant accounting dimensions: M1 is only interesting where value is
delivered progressively over a long-running request, and M2 only where the billed
quantity is a runtime-produced, multi-category vector priced by a category-sensitive
function.

The testbed is a FastAPI gateway over PostgreSQL~16 (\textsc{read committed}) and
Redis~7, with a deterministic mock generator for reproducibility and \emph{real}
tokenizers on the recount path, run under Docker Compose on one commodity host (16
logical CPUs, 16\,GB). Money is exact decimal. Five M1 architectures and six M2
architectures share common interfaces so that experiments compare \emph{semantics}
rather than unrelated code.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
Category-wise pricing over three tiers; leakage \emph{efficiency} (fraction of true value
evaded) is the price-invariant headline metric and absolute dollars are labelled scenario
values. Sweeps: B0 concurrency $\{1,2,5,10,20,30,50\}\times30$ repetitions; M1
architecture $\times$ abort position $\times$ concurrency $\{1,5,20,50,100\}$; M2
architecture $\times$ manipulation $\times$ concurrency. Request volume is held
\emph{constant} across concurrency levels so that volume cannot masquerade as
vulnerability.

\paragraph{Integrity gates.} Every summarizer independently recomputes each record's leak
from primitives, checks invariant-flag consistency, and verifies ledger conservation
$\sum_r N(r)=B_{\text{init}}-B_{\text{final}}$ without trusting the application's own
bookkeeping; a violation fails the build. We validate the gates adversarially: corrupting
a leak value, a debit, a served flag, a balance, an invariant flag, or deleting a ledger
row must each be detected (12/12), alongside metamorphic properties (10/10). The
metamorphic suite also caught one of our own unjustified assumptions --- we had asserted
leakage efficiency was price-tier invariant, but that holds only under \emph{uniform}
price scaling, not under a structural change to the price ratios --- which we corrected
rather than suppressed.

\paragraph{Independent verification.} The strongest M2 defense is re-implemented in a
module sharing no code path with the gateway; verdicts agree on all cross-validated cells
(42 M2, 16 M1, 2 B0). The independent checker initially reported three disagreements,
which were \emph{definitional}, not defects: it aggregated under-payment only, while the
gateway aggregates the signed billing delta. Under flat-total pricing an honest client is
systematically \emph{overcharged}, so the two aggregates legitimately differ. We now
report both, and use under-payment for the security property.

\begin{sloppypar}
\paragraph{Statistics.} All statistics are produced by a standalone script
(scipy/statsmodels) which writes a single output file that the paper cites. Many
conditions here are deterministic by construction and have exactly zero variance; for
those we report the constant and complete separation rather than a p-value, and reserve
nonparametric tests and bootstrap intervals for quantities that genuinely vary.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{B0 --- synchronization (known baseline)}
The non-atomic check-then-decrement leaks only when concurrency $\ge 2$: mean \$-leak is
$0.000$ at $c{=}1$, then $0.099$, $2.871$ and $4.851$ at $c{=}2$, $30$ and $50$, growing
exactly linearly (slope $0.099$ per additional concurrent request, $R^2=1.0000$,
$p=1.9\times10^{-50}$). The hardened atomic compare-and-decrement leaks \$0 at every
level (Table~\ref{tab:b0}, Fig.~\ref{fig:b0}). B0 is not a contribution; it validates the
rig and provides the contrast used below.

\subsection{M1 --- commitment timing}
\label{sec:m1}
Leakage tracks the value delivered before the missing commit, rising monotonically with
abort position and collapsing to zero at completion when the debit finally fires: per
request $0.0045$ at $\sim$1 token delivered, $0.0285$ at 25\%, $0.0540$ at 50\%,
$0.0960$ at 90\%, and $0.0000$ at 100\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:m1}, Table~\ref{tab:m1}).
Request-ASR is $1.00$ at every abort position before completion. The two vulnerable
architectures --- ``debit only after completion'' and ``refund the whole reservation on
abort'' --- are economically identical.

\paragraph{M1 comprises two orthogonal security properties.}
\begin{sloppypar}
\emph{Property A (solvency)}: served value must not exceed the affordable balance.
\emph{Property B (accounting finality)}: delivered value must not exceed the net
committed debit. The ablation separates them (Table~\ref{tab:m1matrix}). With a budget
for two requests and six issued, \texttt{no\_reserve\_settle} preserves accounting
integrity (leak $0.0000$ at every abort position) yet serves all six and drives the
balance to $-0.32$; conversely \texttt{reserve\_refund\_on\_abort} holds a reservation
but refunds it away, failing both. \textbf{Reservation and abort-safe finalization are
orthogonal controls; neither substitutes for the other.}
\end{sloppypar}

\paragraph{Concurrency is not the mechanism.} Under fixed request volume in the tested
architecture, per-request leakage remained unchanged across concurrency levels
$1$--$100$: leak per request measured $0.0528$ at $c=1,5,20,50$ and $100$ (identical to
four decimal places), and request-ASR was exactly $1.000$ (vulnerable) or $0.000$ (safe)
at every level (Table~\ref{tab:m1conc}, Fig.~\ref{fig:m1conc}). The honest-client control
arm leaked zero everywhere. We do not claim invariance as a proven property of all
systems under all loads; we report the measured behaviour of this architecture and, in
\S\ref{sec:discussion}, the structural reason the state model predicts it.

\subsection{M2 --- usage authority}
Writing $U$ for the true usage vector, $P$ for the pricing function and $T$ for the
attacker's transformation of the declared usage, a manipulation pays off exactly when
$P(T(U))<P(U)$ --- that is, when the pricing basis \emph{reads the field $T$ changes}.
Table~\ref{tab:m2formal} derives this per manipulation: a total/subtotal mismatch is inert
under category pricing (which never reads the declared total) and is the strongest attack
under flat-total pricing ($0.741$), while reclassifying output into a discounted cached
category is the reverse. The exploitable set is a property of the billing function, not of
attacker ingenuity. Every server-authoritative architecture drives leakage efficiency to
$0.000$ across all manipulations, and M2 is likewise concurrency-invariant
(Table~\ref{tab:m2conc}).

\paragraph{Usage computation is not usage authority.} This is the central M2 result. The
decisive case is \texttt{client\_logged}, which \emph{computes} a correct server-side
recount, \emph{records} it, and nevertheless continues to use the client-declared number
as the billing basis. Against a 90\% output under-report it leaks at efficiency
$\mathbf{0.583}$ --- indistinguishable from the \texttt{client} architecture that never
recounts at all (Table~\ref{tab:m2}). The ablation reproduces the effect independently
with a different probe prompt, at $0.595$ (Table~\ref{tab:m2abl}); the two differ only
because efficiency depends on the prompt's token mix, and both show the recount changing
nothing. The implication is that
\[
\text{correct usage \emph{computation}} \;\;\not\Rightarrow\;\; \text{correct \emph{billing}},
\]
and the architectural property actually required is that \emph{the authoritative usage
value used for billing must be derived from a trusted source}. Our ablation demonstrates
that recounting without authority does not prevent the observed leakage; in the evaluated
\texttt{client\_logged} architecture the recount provides evidence usable for detection or
post-hoc reconciliation (level D1, \S\ref{sec:detect}) but does not prevent the leak. We
do not claim this holds for every conceivable post-hoc enforcement design --- a system
that acted on the recorded discrepancy before settling would behave differently --- only
that computing the correct number is not by itself sufficient.

\paragraph{Economic sensitivity.} Leakage efficiency is exactly invariant under uniform
price scaling ($\times10$) and shifts by at most $0.012$ under a price-\emph{structure}
change, so the qualitative conclusions are price-independent (Table~\ref{tab:econ}). A
side effect of the same asymmetry is that flat-total pricing systematically
\emph{overcharges} honest clients (efficiency $-0.058$).

\paragraph{Concurrency does not amplify these failures.}
Increased concurrency is not itself a sufficient amplifier of client-side metering
evasion. In the tested architecture, B0 exhibited a concurrency-created race, whereas M1
and M2 remained primarily architectural accounting failures. This negative result follows
a chain we can state explicitly: the \emph{state model predicts} that a defect in
transition \emph{atomicity} depends on contention for shared state, while defects in the
transition \emph{graph} (M1) or in \emph{data authority} (M2) are properties of an
individual request; that prediction yields the architecture-level \emph{hypothesis} that
only B0 should scale with concurrency; the \emph{controlled measurement} holds request
volume constant so that volume cannot masquerade as vulnerability; and the
\emph{observed distinction} matches. Concretely: B0 is concurrency-sensitive because
shared authorization/debit state is raced; M1 is concurrency-independent in the tested
design because the violation lies in the abort path of an individual lifecycle; and M2 is
concurrency-independent in the tested design because the violation lies in the authority
assigned to per-request usage data. Operationally this means an operator cannot infer
safety from low traffic, and cannot detect M1/M2 by watching for load spikes.

\subsection{Cross-architecture validation}
\label{sec:generality}
A measurement study on one deployment invites the objection that its conclusions are
artifacts of that deployment. We therefore repeated the experiments across three
execution topologies and two accounting backends, changing \emph{only} the deployment or
the storage architecture --- the attack harness, the architectures under test, and the
integrity gates are byte-identical across all runs.

\paragraph{Topologies.} (i) \emph{single}: one uvicorn worker, no proxy (the control);
(ii) \emph{multi-worker}: nginx over \textbf{four} uvicorn workers, i.e.\ four independent
event loops sharing one PostgreSQL and one Redis; (iii) \emph{distributed}: an nginx load
balancer over \textbf{two separate gateway containers}, two workers each, again sharing one
datastore. Every ledger row records the serving process, so we can confirm load actually
spread rather than assuming it: four distinct worker processes served M1 requests in the
multi-worker run (101/164/79/136 requests), and both containers served traffic in the
distributed run.

Across \textbf{96} accounting cells compared against the control there were
\textbf{no mismatches} (Table~\ref{tab:topogen}). Differences are either exactly zero or
bounded by the price of a single delivered token --- the disconnect-detection jitter
already characterized in our reproduction audit --- and never change a verdict. B0 is
also preserved: the hardened posture leaks exactly \$0 at every concurrency in every
topology, \emph{including across two separate containers}, because atomicity is enforced
by the database rather than by process locality; and the distributed vulnerable posture
reproduced the control exactly (\$0.8910 at $c{=}10$, \$4.8510 at $c{=}50$).

\paragraph{A multi-worker deployment is not free.} Building the topology exposed two real
defects that a single-worker study cannot reveal, and we report them as findings rather
than silently fixing them. First, the B0 runtime posture was \emph{per-process} state, so
a configuration change would have reached only one of four workers; it must live in
shared state (we moved it to Redis). Second, four workers racing schema creation at boot
crashed with a uniqueness violation on \texttt{pg\_class}; startup must be serialized (we
use a PostgreSQL advisory lock). Neither changes the accounting semantics, but both are
prerequisites for the results above to be meaningful.

\paragraph{Accounting backends.} To test whether the conclusions depend on \emph{how money
is stored}, we implemented a second backend behind the same semantic interface. Backend~A
keeps a mutable \texttt{credits.balance} row and obtains atomicity from a conditional
\texttt{UPDATE {\ldots} WHERE balance >= cost}. Backend~B never mutates that row: every
movement is an immutable row in an append-only ledger and the balance is \emph{derived} as
the sum of those entries, with the atomic reserve becoming a conditional append serialized
on an anchor row. Running the strongest B0/M1/M2 cases against both,
\textbf{17 of 17 were byte-identical} --- same leakage, same leakage efficiency, same
invariant-violation counts, same reconciliation verdicts (Table~\ref{tab:backendgen}).
This supports the structural claim: the outcomes follow from where synchronization,
commitment and authority sit, not from the storage representation.

\subsection{Lifecycle failure modes}
Beyond the scripted attack we inject nine lifecycle failures (disconnect at $t{=}0$ and
mid-stream, truncated stream, settle-window race, missing / delayed / duplicated usage
metadata, and a recount-engine failure). Every invariant violation falls on a vulnerable
architecture; the hardened architectures survive all of them; and a recount-engine failure
is \emph{fail-closed} --- the request is rejected and nothing is served or billed
(Table~\ref{tab:failmodes}).

\subsection{Detectability}
\label{sec:detect}
Detectability is orthogonal to exploitability: a failure can be exploitable and hidden,
exploitable but reconcilable, or prevented outright. We classify D0 (no evidence), D1
(post-hoc reconciliation only), D2 (logged discrepancy), D3 (prevented in real time).
Under \texttt{client} billing the leak is D0 --- the ledger faithfully records the
client's lie. Merely \emph{logging} a recount moves the same leak to D1 without preventing
it, and reconciling in-request reaches D3 (Table~\ref{tab:detect}). This gives operators
who cannot yet re-architect billing a cheap intermediate step.

\section{Defense Sufficiency Conditions}
\label{sec:sufficiency}
The ablation shows which primitive is load-bearing empirically; here we state, for each
mechanism, conditions that are \emph{sufficient within the accounting model} to guarantee
$V(r)\le N(r)$. These are model-level deductive arguments, \textbf{not} machine-checked
proofs and \textbf{not} universal theorems about deployed systems; we separate what the
model forces from what our implementation happens to supply.

\paragraph{M1 --- commitment timing.} Sufficient: \emph{(M1-a) terminal-path totality} ---
every terminal path, including client disconnect, server exception and stream truncation,
reaches a state where an accounting event for $r$ is committed and durable; and
\emph{(M1-b) refund boundedness} --- every refund satisfies
$F(r)\le \mathrm{Res}(r)-V(r)$. If a reservation was taken, (M1-b) gives
$N(r)=\mathrm{Res}(r)-F(r)\ge V(r)$; if not, (M1-a) forces the terminal event to commit
$D(r)=V(r)$. Solvency is a \emph{separate} condition \emph{(M1-c)}: reserve atomically
before execution and refuse the request when the reservation fails.
\texttt{no\_reserve\_settle} satisfies (M1-a)/(M1-b) but not (M1-c), and measured exactly
that --- integrity held (leak $0.0000$) while the balance went to $-0.32$.

\paragraph{M2 --- usage authority.} Sufficient: \emph{(M2-a) authority} --- the usage value
used for billing is derived from state the attacker cannot control, i.e.\
$U_{\text{bill}}=U_{\text{true}}$; and \emph{(M2-b) pricing monotonicity} --- $P$ is
non-decreasing in each category. Computing $U_{\text{true}}$ is \emph{not} sufficient: an
architecture that computes it correctly, records it, and bills $P(U_{\text{decl}})$
satisfies ``the server recounts'' while violating (M2-a), and leaks
$P(U_{\text{true}})-P(T(U_{\text{true}}))$.

\paragraph{B0 --- synchronization.} Sufficient: \emph{(B0-a)} the check
$\text{balance}\ge\text{cost}$ and the decrement occur as one operation atomic with
respect to competing transactions. Under (B0-a) the authorizations on an account are
totally ordered, so no two requests authorize against the same funds. With a single
in-flight transaction (B0-a) holds vacuously, which is why B0 leaks nothing at
concurrency~1.

\paragraph{Implementation-specific caveats.} The model says ``committed and durable''; our
implementation achieves it with a cancellation-shielded settlement task, and a framework
that cancelled that task would satisfy the architecture on paper while violating (M1-a).
The \texttt{upstream} architecture satisfies (M2-a) \emph{only because the provider is
honest by assumption}. And (B0-a) is a property, not a mechanism: our two backends obtain
it by a conditional \texttt{UPDATE} and by an anchor-row lock respectively, which is
precisely what \S\ref{sec:generality} tests. Full statements, arguments and caveats are in
the accompanying \texttt{defense\_sufficiency} document.

\section{Defense Evaluation}
Each vulnerable architecture is paired with a server-side primitive: atomic
compare-and-decrement (B0), reserve-then-abort-safe-finalization (M1), and
server-authoritative recount (M2). Under every defense the invariant-violation rate and
leakage efficiency are zero across the full attack catalogue.

\paragraph{What the strongest defense costs.} We measure recount at three levels because
they answer different questions.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Standalone tokenizer} (Fig.~\ref{fig:toklat}): cost is linear in input
length (log--log slope $0.999$--$1.14$, $R^2\ge0.998$), so it budgets as
tokens\,$\times$\,a per-token constant ($\approx$0.7\,\textmu s/token for tiktoken,
$\approx$2.5\,\textmu s/token for SentencePiece). At 16{,}384 tokens the engines span
$10.1$--$41.8$\,ms, a $2.9$--$3.6\times$ implementation effect; at a fixed token count,
workload changes cost by up to $3.4\times$.
\item \textbf{Gateway with a \emph{mock} generator}: recount is nearly free at
$\le$1{,}024 tokens ($\ge0.96\times$ throughput) but costs heavily at 16{,}384 tokens
($0.05$--$0.76\times$ at $c\!\in\!\{1,10\}$). Tokenization time explains this
(Spearman $\rho=-0.868$, $p=1.3\times10^{-5}$).
\item \textbf{Local real-model experiment}: with real autoregressive generation the
recount is $\mathbf{0.014\%}$--$\mathbf{0.019\%}$ of end-to-end time across 128-, 1024-
and 4096-token contexts, and measured throughput ratios scatter around $1.0$ with signs
that flip across workloads, i.e.\ within a $\pm$10\% noise band that is itself
$\sim$700$\times$ larger than the effect.
\end{enumerate}
The apparent expense in (2) is an artifact of the mock generator costing \emph{nothing}:
when generation is free, tokenization is the entire request. We report all three rather
than the most convenient one, and we do \emph{not} claim recount is ``essentially free'':
it is a real, predictable cost that is small \emph{relative to real inference} in the
tested configuration.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Three dimensions of one accounting-security boundary behave differently under load, and
the difference is predicted by \emph{which} element of the request lifecycle is
defective: transition atomicity (B0) is a shared-resource property and was therefore
concurrency-created, whereas the transition graph (M1) and data authority (M2) are
per-request properties and showed no concurrency dependence in the tested design. The
practical consequences are that (i) an operator cannot use traffic level as a proxy for
exposure, (ii) integrity and solvency require different controls, and (iii) in the
evaluated architectures a correct recount did not prevent leakage unless it was
authoritative for billing. The defenses themselves are ordinary; the contribution is
knowing precisely which one is load-bearing and what it costs.

\paragraph{``Is this not just CWE-807 plus a race plus known streaming bugs?''}
This is the objection we consider most likely, and it is partly correct --- which is why
we state it ourselves. B0 \emph{is} a known generic race and is presented as a baseline,
not a contribution. The enabling mechanisms behind M1 and M2 are individually known:
trusting client-supplied values is CWE-807, and disconnect-related accounting gaps appear
in a deployed gateway issue~\cite{newapi5235}, an operations writeup, and practitioner
guidance~\cite{practice}. \textbf{We claim no novelty at the primitive level.} What the
paper adds is: (i) a unified accounting model in which these otherwise-unrelated failures
are the same integrity property violated at different points of one request lifecycle;
(ii) a taxonomy that separates synchronization, lifecycle commitment, and usage authority
as \emph{experimentally distinguishable} dimensions --- the concurrency contrast in
\S\ref{sec:results} is evidence that the separation is real rather than editorial;
(iii) an M1 ablation showing that solvency and accounting integrity are orthogonal and
require different primitives, which is not derivable from ``there is a streaming bug'';
(iv) an M2 ablation isolating computation from authority, where an architecture that
recounts correctly still leaks $0.583$; and (v) a common measurement --- economic leakage,
leakage efficiency, detectability, invariant violations, and defense overhead --- applied
uniformly across all three dimensions, which no single prior source provides. We do not
assert that practitioners believed a recount was sufficient; we show experimentally that
in the evaluated architecture it was not.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\paragraph{Internal.} Single uvicorn worker, so gateway recount numbers are an upper
bound; shared host and Docker scheduling; local loopback adds a $\sim$45\,ms floor per
request on the Windows/Docker Desktop configuration, which makes the gateway ratios
conservative for small inputs; mock generation everywhere except the real-model
experiment; leakage that depends on delivered tokens is sensitive to disconnect timing at
high concurrency (the within-cell standard deviation of delivered tokens is exactly zero
at $c\le20$ and $\approx$0.011 at $c\in\{50,100\}$).

\paragraph{Two different high-concurrency questions.} It is important to separate
\emph{accounting-integrity measurements} from \emph{gateway performance measurements},
because only the latter are affected here.

\emph{(a) Accounting integrity} was measured at $c=1,5,20,50$ and $100$ and \textbf{none
of it was discarded}: leakage, leakage efficiency, request-ASR and invariant violations
are deterministic functions of the ledger, not of throughput, and every trial passed
ledger conservation at every concurrency level.

\emph{(b) Gateway throughput ratios} at $c\ge50$ are excluded from headline performance
claims because the benchmark host became the limiting factor. The repeat spread reaches
17\% of the median, the no-recount baseline is itself non-monotonic, and single runs
produced ratios $>1$ --- physically impossible for strictly-added work --- which is what
motivated the switch to median-of-three repeats. These are symptoms of single-worker
server and client/event-loop saturation, not of recount cost. Only $c=1$ and $c=10$ are
treated as performance evidence; the excluded cells remain published in
\texttt{results/} and summarized here rather than removed.

\paragraph{Construct.} Synthetic price tiers; a token-count abstraction of ``inference
value''; leakage normalized by delivered value; and a detectability scale that separates
exploitability from evidence availability.

\paragraph{External.} The cross-architecture experiments (\S\ref{sec:generality}) remove
the single-event-loop, single-process and single-storage-design confounds, but they do not
remove all of them, and we state the boundary precisely. \emph{Addressed:} four
independent event loops; two separate gateway containers behind a load balancer; and two
structurally different accounting backends (mutable row vs.\ append-only derived ledger).
\emph{Not addressed:} all instances still run on \textbf{one physical host} sharing
\textbf{one} PostgreSQL and \textbf{one} Redis, so multi-host networking, replica lag,
cross-region latency and datastore partitioning are untested; both backends are
strongly-consistent single-datastore designs, so an \textbf{eventually-consistent} or
sharded accounting pipeline --- which could break the atomicity condition (B0-a) or the
durability condition (M1-a) --- remains unevaluated. We also test one model family, one
cache, and no commercial provider; the real-model experiment bounds a \emph{ratio} in one
configuration and does not validate any commercial provider's billing behaviour.

\paragraph{Formal.} The sufficiency conditions of \S\ref{sec:sufficiency} are model-level
deductive arguments, not machine-checked proofs. We do not claim they are sufficient in an
arbitrary deployed system, nor that no other sufficient condition set exists. Their
\emph{necessity} evidence is empirical: removing a condition produced a measured violation
in our implementation.

\paragraph{Literature.} A targeted search rather than a proof of absence, in a
fast-moving area with terminology mismatch risk.

\section{Responsible Disclosure and Ethics}
This work produced no new vulnerability findings against any third-party, live, or
production system; all experiments ran against the local testbed shipped with the
artifact, whose harness defaults to \texttt{localhost} and contains no third-party
endpoints. No credentials, real user data, or production billing APIs were used. The
public artifacts we cite as prior evidence (a GitHub issue~\cite{newapi5235}, a
PR~\cite{aperture247}, and a CVE~\cite{cve2026}) were public before this work and are not
our findings. Every attack in the artifact is paired with a defense, and the testbed is
released so operators can measure their own gateways.

\section{Conclusion}
Client-side under-payment in LLM metering is best modelled as one accounting boundary
with three placements: synchronization, commitment timing, and usage authority.
Localizing each to a state transition predicts --- and our measurements confirm --- that
only the synchronization defect is concurrency-created, that integrity and solvency are
closed by different primitives, and that a server-side recount must be authoritative
rather than merely computed. In the tested configuration that defense costs a small,
predictable fraction of real inference time.
